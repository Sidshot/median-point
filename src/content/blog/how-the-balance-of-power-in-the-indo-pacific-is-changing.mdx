  The unipolar moment in the Pacific has definitively ended. According to the 2025 Lowy Institute Asia Power Index, the United States recorded its lowest ever comprehensive power score of 80.5—a decline of 1.2 points from previous assessments. This deterioration is concentrated in precisely the domains that underpin traditional military deterrence: defence networks, where the United States lost 3.6 points, and economic capability, where it lost 2.4 points. These are not marginal fluctuations. They represent a structural erosion of American advantages that had seemed immutable for decades.

  <CustomHTML code={'<iframe src="https://power.lowyinstitute.org/embed/map" width="100%" height="600" frameborder="0"></iframe>'} />

  China's trajectory represents the inverse. Ranked second with a comprehensive score of 73.7, the People's Republic gained one full point in 2025, narrowing the gap with the United States to a historic low of under seven points. While China suffers its own vulnerabilities—most notably a significant 4.7-point drop in defence networks and a 2.5-point decline in economic relationships—its military capability and cultural influence continue to surge. The narrowing gap fundamentally alters the strategic calculus of every state in the region.

  The most consequential development in the 2025 rankings, however, is India's elevation. Achieving a comprehensive power score of 40.0, India officially surpassed Japan, which fell to 38.8, to become the third most powerful state in the Indo-Pacific. This is not a symbolic shift. India now possesses sufficient weight to serve as a credible swing state—capable of tilting the regional balance through strategic alignment while retaining the institutional autonomy to resist complete subordination to either Washington or Beijing.

  The cumulative effect of these shifts is profound. A power gap that once ensured unassailable American dominance has narrowed to the point where regional governments can no longer assume US superiority. From Tokyo to New Delhi to Jakarta, every capital must now calculate how to navigate a theatre where the United States retains structural advantages but no longer possesses unchallenged hegemony. The empirical reality is clear: the Indo-Pacific is increasingly multipolar, and the old certainties of the post-Cold War order have given way to a fluid, contested strategic environment.

## **KINETIC ESCALATION AND THE SECOND THOMAS SHOAL**

The abstract numbers of the Lowy Index translate into terrifying kinetic reality in the South China Sea. At the Second Thomas Shoal, a submerged reef in the Spratly Islands, China has abandoned the maritime norms that previously governed contested waters. What was once a theatre of calibrated maritime law enforcement has become a laboratory for extreme coercion, testing the limits of American alliance commitments while demonstrating to every Southeast Asian state that Beijing possesses both the capability and willingness to employ visceral force.

<Tweet id="https://x.com/Joseph_Morong/status/1803423751960404000" />

The Philippines maintains a marine detachment aboard the BRP Sierra Madre, a deliberately grounded World War II-era naval vessel that serves as a forward operating post and territorial marker. This positioning requires constant resupply missions from the Philippine Navy—missions that China has systematically sought to interdict. For years, Chinese tactics remained within the bounds of maritime law enforcement: water cannons, aggressive manoeuvring, and ramming attempts designed to deny access without causing direct physical harm to personnel. That restraint ended on 17 June 2024.

On that date, China Coast Guard personnel intercepted a Philippine resupply mission with unprecedented violence. Armed with bladed weapons and axes, Chinese sailors punctured Philippine rigid hull inflatable boats and seized equipment. The confrontation resulted in a severed thumb for a Philippine Navy officer—a visceral escalation that pushed the conflict perilously close to the threshold of an "armed attack" that could trigger the 1951 US-Philippines Mutual Defense Treaty. The shift from water cannons to hand-to-hand combat was not incidental. It was a deliberate signal that Beijing would escalate beyond the established norms of maritime confrontation to achieve its territorial objectives.

> The strategic logic behind this escalation is clear. China is systematically testing how far it can push before the United States responds. The Second Thomas Shoal has become a laboratory for gray-zone warfare—operations that achieve territorial objectives through calibrated force while remaining just below the threshold of conventional military conflict. By using paramilitary rather than naval forces, Beijing maintains plausible deniability. By inflicting injury rather than fatalities, it avoids triggering formal treaty obligations. Each incident expands the acceptable boundaries of Chinese coercion while degrading the credibility of American deterrent threats.

The violence did not produce a lasting resolution. In July 2024, Manila and Beijing brokered a classified "provisional agreement" enabling subsequent resupply missions to proceed without kinetic interception. Yet the underlying sovereign dispute remains utterly unresolved. Interpretations of the agreement differ sharply between the two capitals, and the shoal remains a highly volatile flashpoint. The incident illustrates China's willingness to utilise extreme coercion while maintaining sufficient ambiguity to avoid formal treaty activation. For the United States and its allies, the Second Thomas Shoal represents a warning: Beijing will escalate, it will test, and it will continue to probe for the limits of American resolve.

### **ERASING THE MEDIAN LINE IN THE TAIWAN STRAIT**

If the Second Thomas Shoal demonstrates China's willingness to escalate in the maritime domain, the Taiwan Strait demonstrates its capacity for systemic strategic pressure. The People's Liberation Army Air Force has systematically dismantled the historical norms that once governed the strait—most notably the median line that had served as an implicit boundary between Chinese and Taiwanese-controlled airspace since the Cold War. This line, once a respected geopolitical marker, has ceased to exist in practice.

<Tweet id="https://x.com/ianellisjones/status/1803176602567840181" />

The data is unequivocal. In January 2026 alone, the PLAAF conducted 166 sorties across the median line. While this represented a seasonal winter decline from peak incursion rates observed in previous months, the sustained volume confirms a permanent operational shift. The crossing of the median line is no longer an international crisis; it is the new, normalised baseline of PLA operations. The Taiwanese military, once able to anticipate and respond to incursions as exceptional events, now confronts a near-constant aerial presence that degrades both its operational capacity and its psychological resilience.

The strategic objectives behind this normalisation are twofold. Operationally, the PLAAF is compressing the timeline for United States early-warning intervention to near zero. American forces previously possessed sufficient warning time to position assets, coordinate with allies, and project credible deterrent capability in response to significant Chinese military movements. The erasure of the median line has fundamentally degraded this response window. By maintaining a persistent presence, Beijing ensures that any escalation—from a blockade to an amphibious assault—can occur with minimal notice, reducing Washington's ability to mount a timely conventional response.

Psychologically, the PLA is systematically eroding Taiwanese resolve. By making incursions routine, Beijing habituates Taiwanese citizens and military personnel to a reality in which Chinese military dominance is the baseline rather than the exception. Each sortie reinforces Taiwan's strategic vulnerability while demonstrating the PLA's capacity to escalate from patrol to blockade to assault without significant operational friction. The objective is to exhaust Taiwanese airframes, degrade pilot morale, and convince the population that resistance is futile. This is not merely military pressure; it is a campaign of psychological attrition designed to fracture Taiwanese will before any actual invasion.

The PLA's Joint Sword exercises further underscore this strategic intent. These large-scale exercises integrate naval, air, and ground forces in complex amphibious and anti-access operations designed to simulate the isolation and neutralisation of Taiwanese defences. The exercises are not merely training; they are rehearsals for a contingency that Beijing increasingly regards as inevitable. By combining sustained incursions with periodic large-scale exercises, China is creating a continuum of pressure that leaves Taiwan with no respite and no clear threshold at which American intervention would be triggered. The median line is gone. The question is no longer whether China will escalate, but when.

### **THE PROCUREMENT PARADOX AND THE DEVELOPMENTAL CUL-DE-SAC**

The United States is not losing to China because of insufficient ambition or inadequate funding. It is losing because of structural dysfunction in its defence procurement system—a dysfunction that prioritises the hypothetical weapons of tomorrow over the operational requirements of today.

In October 2025, the Center for a New American Security published a devastating critique of the United States defence budget request titled "Stuck in the Cul-de-Sac." The report's central finding is that the Pentagon has become trapped in what its authors describe as a "developmental cul-de-sac"—a budgetary orientation that systematically privileges long-term Research, Development, Test and Evaluation at the direct expense of near-term procurement. The Department of Defense is funding the military of 2035 while starving the force required in 2026.

The data is stark. The FY 2025 and FY 2026 defence budget requests demonstrate a systematic misallocation of resources. The Pentagon is heavily overinvesting in future systems that will not be operationally ready for years—next-generation fighters, hypersonic missiles, advanced naval platforms, and directed-energy weapons—while delaying or reducing procurement of ready-to-fight platforms like the F-15EX fighter and the B-21 bomber. The result is a shrinking, ageing Joint Force that lacks the immediate capacity to deter Chinese adventurism within the First Island Chain.

The strategic implications are profound. By funding long-term innovation at the expense of immediate combat credibility, the Pentagon is actively degrading its capacity to fulfil its security commitments in the Western Pacific. The United States cannot credibly threaten conventional escalation to deter Chinese aggression because its forces lack the readiness and numbers to execute such a strategy. Beijing understands this and is systematically exploiting the window of vulnerability.

This structural vulnerability is not merely bureaucratic; it is geostrategic. The United States has committed to defending Taiwan, the Philippines, Japan, South Korea, and other regional allies against Chinese coercion. Its defence budget, however, systematically undermines its capacity to fulfil these commitments. The gap between stated strategic objectives and actual budgetary execution represents a fundamental crisis of American credibility in the Indo-Pacific. Every regional capital now calculates whether Washington's promises are backed by sufficient force to be credible. Increasingly, the answer appears to be no.

The CNAS report warns that this "developmental cul-de-sac" creates a dangerous window of conventional vulnerability that China will exploit to alter the territorial status quo. By the time the next-generation systems arrive, the strategic landscape may have shifted irreversibly. Beijing is not waiting for the United States to fix its procurement problems. It is acting now, confident that the window of American weakness will remain open for years. This is not a failure of military technology; it is a failure of procurement strategy, and it is handing China a decisive advantage in the race for regional dominance.

### **THE GEOECONOMIC COUNTER-OFFENSIVE: TRUST AND PAX SILICA**

---

Unable to reverse the People's Liberation Army's localized kinetic dominance in the short term, the United States is executing a massive counter-offensive in a different domain: technology. If Washington cannot match Chinese naval mass ship-for-ship in the Western Pacific, it will construct an impenetrable macroeconomic fortress that denies Beijing access to the artificial intelligence compute and semiconductor infrastructure required for next-generation economic growth. This is not a secondary strategy; it is the primary American response to the procurement crisis documented in the previous section.

In February 2025, President Trump and Prime Minister Modi formally announced the [TRUST initiative](https://www.psa.gov.in/icet)—Transforming the Relationship Utilizing Strategic Technology. This framework superseded the Biden administration's Initiative on Critical and Emerging Technologies, retaining the focus on artificial intelligence, quantum computing, and semiconductors while applying a highly transactional, results-oriented methodology designed to accelerate private-sector infrastructure deployment and data centre construction. TRUST represents a fundamental shift in United States-India strategic alignment, moving from consultative dialogue to concrete industrial integration.&#x20;

<ImageCaption src="https://in.usembassy.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/241/2026/01/DSC09580-768x512.jpg" alt="U.S. Consul General Laura Williams addresses participants at the TRUST Conference in Hyderabad" caption="U.S. Consul General Laura Williams addresses participants at the TRUST Conference in Hyderabad" credit="US Embassy" fullWidth={false} />

India's strategic utility to Washington is immense. As the third most powerful state in the Indo-Pacific, India serves as the geographic and economic anchor for efforts to diversify supply chains away from China. By integrating Indian institutions into the American technology ecosystem, Washington is creating alternative production networks that reduce dependence on Chinese manufacturing while leveraging India's demographic advantages and scientific capabilities. The TRUST initiative formalises a relationship that had previously existed only in strategic documents.

The TRUST initiative, however, is merely the first layer of a larger containment architecture. The second layer—and the more consequential—arrived in December 2025 with the formal launch of the Pax Silica coalition. Pax Silica represents the most aggressive United States-led effort to date to secure the industrial foundations of artificial intelligence and semiconductor manufacturing. Initially comprising seven founding signatories, the coalition expanded rapidly to sixteen nations by mid-2026, encompassing critical technology nodes such as Japan, South Korea, the Netherlands, Taiwan, the United Kingdom, and, critically, India.

The coalition's explicit geopolitical objective is to eliminate Chinese over-concentration in critical supply chains and prevent economic coercion. The initiative is backed by a $250 million seed fund designed to leverage up to $1 trillion in investments from state-linked sovereign wealth funds and private capital. Pax Silica also deploys the "Edge AI Package for the Indo-Pacific," providing funding for telecommunications operators to develop alternatives to Chinese technology. The financial architecture is designed to create self-sustaining ecosystems that outcompete Chinese alternatives while locking allied nations into American-dominated supply chains.

On 20 February 2026, at the AI Impact Summit in New Delhi, India formally joined Pax Silica as its twelfth member. This signalled a massive geopolitical realignment of semiconductor supply chains. India's accession to the coalition firmly integrates New Delhi into the United States-led semiconductor and AI containment architecture, binding Indian economic interests to American technological leadership. The strategic calculus is clear: by making India a stakeholder in the coalition's success, Washington ensures that New Delhi has a direct interest in excluding Chinese technology from regional markets.

However, Pax Silica's physical footprint has generated significant controversy. In April 2026, the Philippines joined the coalition, agreeing to host a massive 4,000-acre industrial hub at the former Clark Air Base. Yet this exposed the imperial undertones of the American-led initiative, as Washington demanded United States common law jurisdiction and diplomatic immunity within the zone. These demands sparked intense sovereignty disputes in Manila, with Philippine nationalists objecting to the de facto extraterritoriality of the American-controlled facility. The Clark hub controversy illustrates the fundamental tension at the heart of Pax Silica: the coalition is simultaneously a voluntary alliance of sovereign states and an American-led technology empire. The success of the initiative depends on Washington's ability to balance its security imperatives with the sovereign sensitivities of its partners. Failure to resolve these tensions could fracture the coalition before it achieves its strategic objectives.

### **THE LIMITS OF ALIGNMENT: TARIFFS AND THE INTERIM TRADE AGREEMENT**

---

The greatest threat to the emerging Indo-Pacific containment architecture is not Beijing. It is Washington's own protectionist instincts. Despite the high-tech alignment achieved through the TRUST initiative and Pax Silica, the United States and India remain trapped in a pattern of macroeconomic hostility that threatens to fracture the very coalition both nations are attempting to build. Strategic alignment in semiconductors and artificial intelligence does not automatically erase deeply entrenched domestic protectionism.

From 22 to 24 June 2026, United States Trade Representative Jamieson Greer met with Indian Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal in New Delhi to finalise an Interim Trade Agreement. The negotiations were intended to resolve the persistent trade disputes that have poisoned the bilateral economic relationship for years—disputes over agricultural market access, digital services taxation, pharmaceutical intellectual property, and tariff barriers that have hindered trade growth despite political goodwill. Instead, the talks exposed the deep structural friction that persists despite technological cooperation.

<Tweet id="https://x.com/ANI/status/2069766678570041347/video/1" />

The two sides failed to produce a signed agreement. While both governments noted "substantial progress" in public statements, no final document emerged from the negotiations. This failure is highly consequential due to a looming 24 July 2026 deadline, when temporary 10 per cent tariffs—imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act after the United States Supreme Court struck down broader executive tariffs earlier in the year—are set to expire. The expiration threatens to plunge the bilateral relationship into renewed commercial chaos, with Indian exporters facing sudden cost increases that could disrupt supply chains and undermine the very economic integration that TRUST and Pax Silica were designed to foster.

The disconnect between technological alignment and macroeconomic hostility is stark. At the highest tiers of strategic technology—semiconductors, artificial intelligence, quantum computing, and defence co-production—the United States and India are seamlessly integrating. Indian engineers contribute to American semiconductor design; American capital flows into Indian data centres; both nations collaborate on next-generation defence platforms. Yet in traditional macroeconomic trade, the relationship remains defined by brinkmanship and mutual suspicion. American agricultural producers demand tariff reductions; Indian pharmaceutical manufacturers demand intellectual property protections; both sides accuse the other of unfair trade practices.

The Greer-Goyal negotiations reveal the limits of strategic alignment. Technological cooperation does not automatically override domestic political pressures. American industries demand tariff barriers to protect domestic manufacturing from Indian competition. Indian industries demand market access to sustain export growth. These competing demands create friction that transcends strategic imperatives. When trade negotiators sit across the table, the high-minded rhetoric of strategic partnership gives way to the brutal arithmetic of industrial competition.

The contradiction is not limited to the United States-India relationship. The Pax Silica coalition itself is vulnerable to protectionist pressure. As the coalition expands, member states will demand reciprocal economic benefits. When those benefits fail to materialise—or when American trade policies impose costs on allied economies—coalition cohesion will degrade. The Philippines, already sensitive to United States demands for diplomatic immunity at the Clark Air Base industrial hub, will expect tangible economic returns for its sovereignty concessions. Japan and South Korea, both heavily dependent on exports to the United States, will resist any American trade measures that threaten their domestic industries.

The strategic implication is clear: the United States cannot build a durable containment architecture if it simultaneously alienates its allies through protectionist trade policies. Strategic alignment in technology cannot substitute for macroeconomic stability. The tech alliance is highly fragile if it cannot be undergirded by stable, predictable trade relationships. Every tariff dispute, every market access negotiation, every intellectual property conflict chips away at the trust that sustains the coalition. Washington must recognise that its Indo-Pacific strategy depends as much on trade policy as on defence procurement or technological innovation. Without a stable economic foundation, the containment architecture will collapse.

The Greer-Goyal negotiations represent a test case. If the United States and India cannot resolve their trade differences despite the strategic imperative of containing China, what hope is there for broader coalition cohesion? The failure to finalise the Interim Trade Agreement suggests that old habits of protectionism die hard—and that the new alliances of the Indo-Pacific remain vulnerable to the same commercial pressures that have historically divided nations.

### **STRATEGIC OUTCOMES AND REGIONAL BIFURCATION**

---

The evidence reviewed in this investigation points to a permanent bifurcation of the Indo-Pacific strategic environment—a division that will define the region's security architecture for the foreseeable future. The old unipolar order, in which the United States exercised undisputed maritime dominance, has given way to a more complex and contested landscape in which military power and technological influence operate in distinct, increasingly disconnected spheres.

<ImageCaption src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a1/Geographic_Boundaries_of_the_First_and_Second_Island_Chains.png" alt="Geographic Boundaries of the First and Second Island Chains" caption="Geographic Boundaries of the First and Second Island Chains" credit="Wikipedia" fullWidth={false} />

Within the [First Island Chain,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_island_chain) China is achieving localized kinetic dominance that is rapidly becoming irreversible. The People's Liberation Army has normalised military incursions across the Taiwan Strait median line, eroding Taiwanese psychological resilience and compressing United States early-warning intervention windows to near zero. The median line no longer exists as a meaningful boundary; it has been replaced by a persistent Chinese aerial presence that degrades Taiwanese defensive capacity and reduces American response options. Further south, Chinese Coast Guard vessels have escalated gray-zone tactics at the Second Thomas Shoal to hand-to-hand combat using bladed weapons, demonstrating Beijing's willingness to employ extreme coercion in pursuit of territorial objectives. The Philippines, despite its mutual defence treaty with the United States, has been forced to accept a fragile provisional agreement that does not resolve the underlying sovereignty dispute.

The structural procurement dysfunction documented in the Center for a New American Security report confirms that this window of Chinese dominance will remain open for the foreseeable future. The United States Department of Defense is trapped in a developmental cul-de-sac, prioritising long-term research and development over immediate procurement of ready-to-fight platforms. By the time the next-generation systems arrive—advanced fighters, hypersonic missiles, and next-generation naval vessels—the strategic landscape may have shifted irreversibly. Beijing understands this and is systematically exploiting the window of American weakness, establishing new territorial realities through calibrated force while Washington struggles to close the procurement gap.

<ImageCaption src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a7/Pacific_Basin_Island_Geography.jpg" alt="Second Island chain" caption="Second Island chain" credit="wikimedia" fullWidth={false} />

However, the strategic picture beyond the First Island Chain is dramatically different. The United States and its partners are successfully dominating the macroeconomic and technological architecture of the broader Indo-Pacific. The TRUST initiative and Pax Silica coalition represent a grand strategic pivot—substituting silicon for ships, embedding regional swing states into an exclusive technology ecosystem that denies Beijing access to the artificial intelligence compute and semiconductor infrastructure required for next-generation economic growth. If China is winning the battle for geography within the First Island Chain, the United States is winning the battle for the technological future of the broader region.

India's strategic pivot is critical to this architecture. By elevating India to the third-ranking power in the region and integrating it into United States-led technology coalitions, Washington has created a counterweight to Chinese economic influence that extends far beyond military capability. India's demographic advantages, scientific capacity, and geographic position make it the ultimate regional swing state—a nation capable of tilting the balance of power through strategic alignment while maintaining sufficient institutional autonomy to resist complete subordination to either Washington or Beijing. The TRUST initiative and India's accession to Pax Silica represent a fundamental realignment of Asian geopolitics, binding New Delhi's economic future to American technological leadership.

The greatest geopolitical risk lies in the short-term window before United States procurement catches up to its technological ambitions. If China achieves a fait accompli in the Taiwan Strait or the South China Sea before Washington can field the platforms necessary to credibly deter aggression, the entire containment architecture collapses. Technological superiority matters little if the physical territory has already been conceded. A blockade of Taiwan, an amphibious assault on the island, or a permanent occupation of contested maritime features would fundamentally alter the strategic environment in ways that technological dominance cannot easily reverse.

<ImageCaption src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ec/Donald_Trump_visit_to_China%2C_May_2026_P20260514DT-1642.jpg" alt="President Donald Trump with Chinese leader Xi Jinping during his state visit to China in 2026" caption="President Donald Trump with Chinese leader Xi Jinping during his state visit to China in 2026" credit="Reuters" fullWidth={false} />

The enduring challenge, however, is managing the internal contradictions of the American-led coalition. Deep-seated trade protectionism threatens to fracture the very alliances Washington is attempting to build. The failure to finalise the United States-India Interim Trade Agreement in June 2026, with looming tariff deadlines approaching, demonstrates how macroeconomic hostility can undermine strategic alignment. If the United States cannot provide its allies with stable economic relationships, strategic alignment in technology may prove insufficient to sustain coalition cohesion. Every tariff dispute, every market access negotiation, every intellectual property conflict chips away at the trust that sustains the containment architecture.

The final outcome remains uncertain, but the trajectory is clear. **The Indo-Pacific is no longer a unipolar American sphere.** It is a bifurcated theatre where China achieves localised kinetic dominance while the United States constructs a global macroeconomic fortress. The balance of power is being rewritten—not through a single decisive confrontation, but through a parallel, decade-long struggle for control of geography and technology. The question is not whether the United States can preserve its Cold War-era dominance; that era has already ended. The question is whether Washington can close its procurement gap before China achieves a territorial fait accompli, and whether the Pax Silica coalition can survive the protectionist pressures that threaten to fragment it. These questions will determine not only the balance of power in the Indo-Pacific but the character of the twenty-first-century international order itself.

The unipolar maritime dominance of the United States in the Indo-Pacific has ended. This is not a prediction; it is an empirical reality confirmed by defence budgets, power indices, and the systematic erosion of territorial norms across the region. Taking advantage of America's structural failure to procure near-term deterrent capabilities, China is successfully normalising kinetic aggression to rewrite territorial realities in the Taiwan Strait and the South China Sea. The median line no longer exists as a meaningful boundary. Second Thomas Shoal has become a laboratory for gray-zone violence. The People's Liberation Army's capacity to escalate—and the United States military's incapacity to deter—have created a new strategic reality in the Western Pacific that no regional government can ignore.

By funding the hypothetical military of 2035 at the direct expense of the force required in 2026, the United States is handing Beijing a multi-year window of operational supremacy within the First Island Chain. China is exploiting this window systematically, establishing new territorial realities through calibrated force while Washington struggles to close the procurement gap. The consequences of this failure are not theoretical; they are visible in the daily incursions across the Taiwan Strait and the violence at Second Thomas Shoal.

Recognising its inability to reverse these localised military disadvantages in the short term, Washington has pivoted to a massive geoeconomic counter-offensive. The TRUST initiative and Pax Silica coalition represent an unprecedented effort to construct an iron curtain around the semiconductor and artificial intelligence supply chains of the twenty-first century. India's elevation to the region's third-ranking power and its integration into American-led technology frameworks have fundamentally altered the strategic calculus of the Indo-Pacific. By making India a stakeholder in the coalition's success, Washington ensures that New Delhi has a direct interest in excluding Chinese technology from regional markets. This is not merely a military strategy; it is a comprehensive effort to shape the technological trajectory of the next half-century.

Yet the containment architecture faces internal threats as serious as external challenges. Deep-seated trade protectionism, as demonstrated by the failure to finalise the United States-India Interim Trade Agreement in June 2026, threatens to fracture the very coalitions Washington is attempting to build. The Greer-Goyal negotiations exposed the enduring tension between high-tech alignment and macroeconomic hostility—a contradiction that will define the future of the American-led alliance system. Strategic alignment in semiconductors and artificial intelligence does not automatically override domestic protectionist pressures. If the United States cannot provide its allies with stable economic relationships, the technological coalition may prove insufficient to sustain alliance cohesion.

> Three essential truths emerge from this investigation. First, China has achieved localised military dominance within the First Island Chain—a dominance that Washington cannot reverse in the short term due to self-inflicted procurement failures. Second, the United States is winning the broader battle for the future through macroeconomic and technological containment—a battle that will define the strategic balance for decades. Third, the ultimate outcome depends not on military capability alone but on Washington's ability to manage the internal contradictions of its coalition while maintaining credible deterrent capacity. The fragmentation of the Pax Silica coalition through protectionist trade policies would be as damaging as any military defeat.

The Indo-Pacific is bifurcating. China is achieving localised kinetic dominance within the First Island Chain. The United States is constructing a global macroeconomic fortress. The struggle between these two visions—one rooted in territorial coercion, the other in technological containment—will define the region's future. Neither side has achieved decisive advantage; both face fundamental vulnerabilities. The outcome will be determined not by any single confrontation but by the cumulative effect of procurement decisions, trade policies, and alliance management over the coming years. The reader should understand that the balance of power is being rewritten in real time—and that the future of the Indo-Pacific remains profoundly uncertain.
