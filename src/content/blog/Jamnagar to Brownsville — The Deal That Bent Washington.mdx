**The Central Question:** Why did one of the world's largest energy companies — Reliance Industries, led by Mukesh Ambani — pour over ₹800 crore into a Texas refinery project that had failed to raise money for years? /

&#x20;For 50 years, a simple rule has ruled American oil: Wall Street banks do not fund new refineries. Building a refinery from scratch costs billions. Permits can take a decade. Lawsuits are almost certain. And profits, when they come, are just a few rupees per litre. The last new refinery built in America started working in 1977. So when a little-known Texas startup called America First Refining announced in early 2026 that it had raised over ₹800 crore, it broke a half‑century trend. The money did not come from an American firm. It came from Reliance Industries — Asia's richest man, Mukesh Ambani, behind it. The plan is to build a $3‑4 billion (roughly ₹25,000‑33,000 crore) refinery at the Port of Brownsville, Texas, near the Mexican border. Reliance has also reportedly signed a 20‑year deal to buy, process, and sell whatever the plant produces. (Sources: ProPublica; corporate announcements; industry reports) In March 2026, the US President went on his social media platform Truth Social to personally thank Reliance for its "tremendous Investment" and called the deal a victory for "AMERICAN ENERGY DOMINANCE."

But here is the puzzle.

Reliance runs the Jamnagar complex in Gujarat — the largest refinery in the world. Jamnagar's profits come from processing heavy, sour crude imported from Russia and Venezuela. America First Refining, however, is designed to process light, sweet shale oil from Texas fracking fields. (Sources: ProPublica; energy industry analysis; CEO interview)

So why would a company that needs heavy crude put over ₹800 crore into a light‑crude project — one that had failed for years to attract any serious investor? That is the simple question this investigation asks: What changed?

> #### The Capital Desert

To understand why the Reliance investment is anomalous, one must first understand how difficult it has been for any new refinery project to secure funding in the United States over the past five decades. Industry analysts have documented that traditional lenders systematically avoid greenfield refining projects due to three interlocking risks: regulatory peril that can delay construction for years, capital intensity that requires billions of dollars before a single barrel is processed, and historically low profit margins that make returns unattractive compared to other energy investments. (Source: Investigative Reporting Battle Manual, citing industry analysis) The Brownsville project embodied all three risks.

* The facility had been proposed under different corporate names for more than seven years before the Reliance investment was announced.
* The Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ) issued its initial air permit to the developers in 2021, but the project repeatedly failed to begin construction.
* The developers requested three separate regulatory extensions from the TCEQ.
* The third and final extension allowable under Texas law was granted on February 26, 2026, pushing the construction start deadline to October 19, 2027. (Source: Inside Climate News, citing TCEQ records)
* Local opposition was sustained and organized. The South Texas Environmental Justice Network, along with a coalition of Cameron County residents known as the Shrimpers and Fishermen of the RGV, filed challenges to the air pollution permits.
* They were represented by attorneys from the Environmental Integrity Project, Texas RioGrande Legal Aid, and Earthjustice.
* More than 2,000 public comments expressed opposition. (Source: Inside Climate News)
* The project’s difficulties were not secret. They were matters of public record.

> #### What Reliance Brought

Despite the project's long struggle, Reliance Industries committed over ₹800 crore. Reliance is no fly‑by‑night investor. In recent years, its annual revenues have crossed $100 billion (over ₹8 lakh crore). Its Jamnagar refinery processes more than 12 lakh barrels of crude every day. Reliance also has deep joint ventures with American companies like Chevron and Pioneer Natural Resources in Texas and Pennsylvania shale fields. It is thoroughly plugged into the U.S. financial system. (Sources: ProPublica; industry records) The investment was structured as a direct infusion into America First Refining — the new name of a company that was earlier called Element Fuels Holdings. As part of the deal, Reliance has also signed a 20‑year agreement to buy more than 80% of whatever the refinery produces.&#x20;

<Tweet id="https://x.com/ANI/status/2031506076693311526" />

On paper, such a long‑term purchase deal is a huge vote of confidence. It suggests the investor believes the project will actually work. But that confidence would have to ignore the project's own documented history of failures, bankruptcies, and false claims.

> #### The Contradiction

The public record does, however, establish a significant timeline: the investment announcement followed a series of meetings involving the President's son, a corporate restructuring that established a new entity registered at the son's residential address, and preceded a sequence of U.S. policy changes that directly benefited Reliance's core business operations.. The industrial logic of this investment does not match Reliance's established refining strategy. Jamnagar's entire business model is built on processing heavy, sour crude — the dense, high‑sulfur oil that comes from Venezuela, Russia, and the Middle East. The facility was specifically engineered to handle this type of crude. Light, sweet shale oil from Texas is a fundamentally different raw material that requires different processing technology.

America First Refining's chief executive, John Calce, has explained this difference himself. In a [March 2026 podcast](https://youtu.be/fUkxicXgp-M), he compared legacy refineries to pizza ovens set at 1,000 degrees, optimised for heavy crude, and his proposed facility to a cookie oven set at 325 degrees, optimised for light shale. (Source: The Jeff Crilley Show transcript)  So here is the strategic puzzle. If Reliance simply wanted access to refined light shale products, it could buy them on global markets without investing ₹800 crore in a brand‑new refinery. If Reliance wanted to expand its own light‑crude processing capacity, it could upgrade its existing facilities at a much lower cost. There is no clear business justification — in any public document — for committing over ₹800 crore to a historically struggling project in a poor border town, led by a chief executive whose previous ventures ended in bankruptcy and fraud allegations.&#x20;

However, the public record does establish a significant timeline. The investment announcement came after a series of meetings involving the US President's son, after a corporate restructuring that created a new company registered at that son's home address, and before a sequence of US policy changes that directly benefited Reliance's core business operations.

> #### The Bankruptcy Docket and the Phantom Infrastructure

In the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Northern District of Texas (Case 19‑32329‑mvl7), a corporate predecessor to Calce’s current venture — Jupiter Marketing & Trading LLC — collapsed into Chapter 7 liquidation; court records show a court‑appointed bankruptcy trustee filed multiple motions to compel turnover of the debtor’s books from Calce, an associate, and a successor entity, alleging that Calce and other insiders had improperly siphoned cash and assets from the failing company. (Source: Bankruptcy court docket; ProPublica) Calce formally denied the allegations, and the case was eventually settled, but the resolution does not erase the record that a federal bankruptcy trustee found sufficient grounds to pursue claims of asset diversion against Calce.&#x20;

&#x20;*Before America First Refining, before Element Fuels, before Jupiter, Calce launched Brownsville Energy Storage Terminals, whose marketing materials claimed a global network of oil storage facilities, an experienced C‑suite, and over 850 employees; subsequent investigative reporting found no evidence to support these claims* — the terminals did not exist, the listed executives were not identifiable as real people, and the contact phone numbers were traced to a taxi service, an obstetrician‑gynecologist’s office, and a baklava caterer. The pattern of unsubstantiated claims about corporate infrastructure is a matter of established fact, though whether that pattern continued under the America First Refining brand remains an open question the public record does not definitively answer. (Source: ProPublica)

![Corporate due-diligence questions surrounding the Brownsville refinery investment](/images/blog/Jamnagar%20to%20Brownsville%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Deal%20That%20Bent%20Washington/10.png)

Reliance Industries is not a retail investor betting on a speculative prospectus but a sophisticated global conglomerate with due diligence resources spanning continents; its joint ventures with Chevron and Pioneer Natural Resources in U.S. shale fields demonstrate its familiarity with the American energy landscape, and its integration with the U.S. financial system subjects it to anti‑money laundering and sanctions compliance regimes that demand thorough counterparty vetting. (Sources: ProPublica; industry records) Whether Reliance conducted such vetting before committing more than $100 million to America First Refining is not disclosed in any public document reviewed for this investigation, and whether Reliance was aware of Calce’s bankruptcy history, the trustee’s allegations of asset diversion, or the fabricated infrastructure claims for Brownsville Energy Storage Terminals is unknown; what is known is that Calce’s history was a matter of public record, accessible through standard due diligence channels.&#x20;

Yet in his media appearances, Calce did not mention the bankruptcy proceedings, the trustee’s allegations, or Brownsville Energy Storage Terminals and its phone numbers routed to a taxi company and a baklava caterer. Instead, he emphasized the difficulty of the permitting process — “It’s hard, as I learned the hard way,” he told the Crilley Show host — praised the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality as “terrific,” and thanked the governor’s office and the National Energy Dominance Council for their support. (Source: The Jeff Crilley Show transcript) He described a 12‑year journey that began in 2015, when he “went down met with the TCEQ” and was told permitting would take 18 months; it took seven years, he said. (Source: The Jeff Crilley Show transcript) The narrative omitted the bankruptcies, the trustee investigations, and the fabricated infrastructure. Whether those omissions constitute material misrepresentations to investors is a legal question beyond the scope of this reporting, but the gap between the public narrative and the documentary record is wide.

The Brownsville refinery project did not lack for regulatory approvals — the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ) issued a modified air permit to the developers in 2021 following contentious administrative hearings and more than 2,000 public comments [(Source: Inside Climate News)](https://insideclimatenews.org/news/12032026/trump-brownsville-texas-oil-refinery/) — but what the project lacked was the capital to begin construction. The developers requested three separate regulatory extensions from the TCEQ; the third and final extension allowable under Texas law was granted on February 26, 2026, less than two months before Calce, in his television interview, said the project would break ground “sometime in April,” pushing the construction deadline to October 19, 2027.  Satellite imagery and on‑the‑ground reporting by the South Texas Environmental Justice Network have shown no substantive site preparation at the Port of Brownsville as of the time of the investment announcement.  A project that had required three extensions to avoid losing its permits, that had shown no visible construction activity, and that was led by a chief executive with a documented history of bankruptcy, trustee investigations, and unsubstantiated corporate claims suddenly attracted more than $100 million from one of the world’s largest energy companies. Yet Calce presents himself as an energy pioneer who has spent 12 years overcoming bureaucratic obstacles, while the court record shows a history of bankruptcy and trustee allegations of asset diversion. He claims the project is shovel‑ready with an April groundbreaking, but regulatory records show the project required three permit extensions and had not begun construction as of spring 2026. And his prior venture, Brownsville Energy Storage Terminals, made claims about employees and infrastructure that were not substantiated by evidence — a pattern that raises questions about the verifiability of his current claims.

> #### THE POLITICAL CONNECTION

The central question is simple: what documented connection exists between the US President's family and the company that received Reliance's money?

In June 2025 — seven months before America First Refining announced its ₹800 crore deal with Reliance — a company called TX Fuels, LLC was registered in the state of Wyoming. Why Wyoming? Because that state allows companies to hide who the real owners are. This is not illegal. Some use it for genuine privacy. But it is also used to keep ownership away from public view. (Source: ProPublica; corporate law analysis) The paperwork was filed by John Willding, a corporate lawyer from Dallas. On LinkedIn, he has openly called himself "outside business counsel to the Trump family." When journalists asked, he confirmed that his job includes "handling the legal paperwork" for family business entities. (Source: State registries; ProPublica)

The incorporation documents for TX Fuels, LLC listed a physical address. That address matched Donald Trump Jr.'s private home in Jupiter, Florida.

Now, the address alone does not prove ownership. An LLC can list a manager's address without the real owner living there. But it certainly ties the entity to a specific place — the home of the President's son.

> #### The Jamnagar Meeting

<YouTube id="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LrxWJqqNAUg" />

In November 2025, Donald Trump Jr. traveled to Jamnagar, India — the home of Reliance Industries‘ largest refining complex — where his host was Anant Ambani, the son of Reliance chairman Mukesh Ambani and a key executive in the conglomerate’s energy division. (Sources: ProPublica; Asia Times) The visit was documented in photographs and media reports: the two men toured the Ambanis‘ private zoo, which houses exotic species, and were photographed performing a traditional Gujarati folk dance together. (Source: Asia Times; ProPublica) Publicly framed as a social and cultural exchange, Indian media reported that Anant Ambani had been working on the Texas refinery deal for months before the visit. (Source: Asia Times) The chronology is precise: the Jamnagar meeting occurred in November 2025; weeks later, in December 2025, the corporate entity then known as Element Fuels Holdings rebranded to America First Refining. (Source: Inside Climate News; Oil and Gas Watch) Whether the rebranding was planned before the Jamnagar meeting or accelerated after it is not established in the public record, but the sequence is documented: meeting, then rebranding, then investment announcement.

Public statements from Trump Jr.‘s representatives have characterized his role in the refinery project as limited, describing him as a “passive minority investor” with “no operational involvement.” (Source: ProPublica) The public record contains information that complicates this characterization: the LLC created to hold his stake was registered by the Trump family’s outside counsel, not by an independent asset manager; the LLC listed Trump Jr.‘s home address, not a corporate office or a blind trust; multiple anonymous sources have told ProPublica that startup executives used Trump Jr.’s involvement to pitch other investors — including a reported meeting in South Florida with Saudi investors — and a foreign government official who was pitched on the project said the company‘s team emphasized the backing of the Trump family and implied that investing would help secure access to the White House. (Source: ProPublica, citing anonymous sources) These claims come from anonymous sources and are included here not as proven facts but as reported allegations that the public record neither confirms nor refutes. What is documented, without reliance on anonymous sources, is that the LLC was created, the address was Trump Jr.’s home, the family lawyer handled the paperwork, and the lawyer publicly celebrated the deal.

*How a Hostile Trade Regime Became a Favorable One — in the Months After a $100 Million Investment*

> **Central Question:** What is the documented sequence of U.S. policy changes affecting Reliance Industries relative to the investment timeline?

![Timeline comparing the Reliance investment with United States policy changes](/images/blog/Jamnagar%20to%20Brownsville%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Deal%20That%20Bent%20Washington/3rd%20image.png)

> #### &#x20;The Venezuelan License (January 2026)

On January 29, 2026, the U.S. Treasury Department’s Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) issued General License 46, authorizing “established U.S. companies and deeply integrated foreign entities” to engage in a broad range of commercial activities involving Venezuelan oil, including the purchase, transport, and financing of crude from the state‑owned PDVSA. (Source: Mayer Brown legal analysis; OFAC public notice) For Reliance Industries, this was a significant strategic victory: the Jamnagar complex requires heavy, sour crude for optimal operation, and Venezuela is one of the world’s largest sources of such crude. But because Reliance operates extensive joint ventures with U.S. companies (including Chevron and Pioneer Natural Resources) and is deeply integrated with the U.S. financial system, it had historically been forced to comply strictly with U.S. sanctions — any violation could have exposed the company to devastating secondary sanctions, cutting it off from dollar‑denominated transactions. (Sources: The Wire India; energy industry analysis) General License 46 removed that barrier, effectively reopening Venezuelan crude to Reliance’s Jamnagar complex and solving a critical supply‑chain constraint. The license was issued less than two months after the November 2025 Jamnagar meeting between Anant Ambani and Donald Trump Jr., and two months before the March 2026 public announcement of Reliance‘s $100 million investment in America First Refining. Whether the license was in development before those events is not established in the public record; the timing, however, is documented.

> #### &#x20;The Tariff Rollback (February 2026)

On February 2, 2026, President Trump announced on social media that the United States had reached an “Interim Agreement” with India, the centerpiece of which was a pledge to roll back the 50 percent tariffs imposed the previous August — reducing them to 18 percent. (Source: Morgan Lewis; White House fact sheet) On February 6, 2026, a revised executive order titled “Modifying Duties to Address Threats to the United States by the Government of the Russian Federation” was issued; effective at 12:01 a.m. on February 7, the order formally terminated the 25 percent ad valorem rate of duty specifically tied to Russian oil purchases, modifying the Harmonized Tariff Schedule by removing headings 9903.01.84 through 9903.01.89. (Source: Federal Register, cited in Morgan Lewis analysis; Morgan Lewis) In exchange for these concessions, India offered a series of purchasing pledges: according to the White House fact sheet, India “intends to purchase” $500 billion worth of U.S. energy products, aircraft parts, precious metals, coking coal, and technology products — specifically graphics processing units (GPUs) — over five years. (Source: White House fact sheet; Morgan Lewis) Market analysts immediately noted the scale of this pledge — a $500 billion commitment over five years would average $100 billion annually, roughly equivalent to the total value of all U.S. goods exports to India in recent years multiplied several times over — with some analysts characterizing the pledge as aspirational rather than binding. (Source: Morgan Lewis analysis) The tariff rollback occurred three months after the Jamnagar meeting and one month before the public investment announcement.

> #### &#x20;The Russian Waivers (April 2026)

![Illustration of sanctions waivers and crude-oil trade flows](/images/blog/Jamnagar%20to%20Brownsville%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Deal%20That%20Bent%20Washington/4.png)

In April 2026, conflict with Iran escalated and the Strait of Hormuz — through which approximately 20 percent of global seaborne oil passes — faced severe disruption; to prevent a global energy price shock, the U.S. Treasury issued rapid, short‑term sanctions waivers allowing India to legally procure Iranian and Russian crude oil that was already in transit or needed to stabilize supplies. (Source: Atlantic Council; NDTV) The waivers were framed as a macroeconomic stabilization measure, but their effect was to provide legal cover for a massive expansion of Russian crude imports by Indian refiners: data from the energy intelligence firm Kpler, cited by The Wire India, showed that Indian refiners imported 2.3 million barrels per day of Russian crude in April 2026 — a record high, led by Reliance Industries. (Source: The Wire India, citing Kpler data) Whether the waivers were issued primarily for macroeconomic reasons or as a specific accommodation to Indian refiners — including Reliance — is not answered by the public record; what is documented is that Reliance received the benefit of the waivers and used them to import Russian crude at record volumes.

> #### The Synchronization

![Chronology aligning corporate events with favorable policy decisions](/images/blog/Jamnagar%20to%20Brownsville%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Deal%20That%20Bent%20Washington/5.png)

The following timeline places the policy changes alongside the corporate events documented in previous sections: The chronology shows that every major policy change favorable to Reliance occurred after the Jamnagar meeting and the corporate rebranding, and either before or concurrent with the public announcement of the investment. Whether the policy changes would have occurred without the investment is not established. Whether the investment would have occurred without the prospect of policy changes is also not established.

> ***What is established is the sequence.***

The administration has stated justifications for each policy change that are facially plausible and grounded in traditional trade and national security logic. On the tariff rollback, the White House has emphasized the reciprocal nature of the deal: India’s pledge to purchase $500 billion in U.S. goods over five years — regardless of its enforceability — provides a conventional trade rationale for reducing tariffs. (Source: White House fact sheet) On the Venezuelan license, OFAC licenses are typically justified on foreign policy grounds; the stated purpose of GL 46 was to support “established U.S. companies” operating in Venezuela, not specifically to benefit Reliance. (Source: Mayer Brown) On the Russian waivers, the Strait of Hormuz crisis created a genuine global supply emergency; issuing waivers to multiple countries, including India, can be defended as a necessary response to prevent oil prices from spiking and fueling U.S. inflation. (Source: Atlantic Council) These rationales mean that even if the policy changes were influenced by the investment, proving causation would require evidence — emails, memoranda, testimony — that is not publicly available.

Yet several contradictions emerge. First, the administration‘s posture toward Reliance shifted from publicly hostile (August 2025) to publicly accommodating (February–March 2026) within a six‑month window that precisely bracketed the Jamnagar meeting and the investment. Second, each policy change addressed a specific strategic pain point for Reliance: heavy crude access (Venezuela), tariff costs (trade deal), and Russian supply continuity (waivers). Third, the $500 billion Indian purchasing pledge that accompanied the tariff rollback is of a magnitude that some analysts have questioned; whether it was a serious commitment or a diplomatic fiction is unclear, but it provided the administration with a defensible public rationale.

> #### The Lobbying Network

Big policy changes like the tariff rollback, the Venezuela oil license, and the Russian waivers don't happen by themselves. Companies hire lobbyists – people who know the right officials, make phone calls, and push for favours. In the months before these policy shifts, both Reliance Industries and the Texas refinery project dramatically increased their spending on such influence operations in Washington. US law requires some disclosure of who is paid and what they are trying to achieve, but the filings only tell part of the story.

> #### Reliance's Washington offensive

Reliance is no stranger to lobbying in Washington. It had hired top law firms before to deal with sanctions and trade rules. In early 2019, facing possible penalties over Venezuelan oil, Reliance retained a big international law firm, listing "US economic trade sanctions policies" as its target. But as the tariff crisis deepened in 2025, Reliance turned to a more powerful player: Ballard Partners. Ballard Partners is a Florida-based lobbying firm with extremely close ties to the Trump administration. Its founder, Brian Ballard, is known as one of the best‑connected lobbyists in Republican politics. The firm has represented foreign governments and big corporations wanting access to the White House. According to Senate disclosure forms, Reliance – through its US subsidiary RIL USA – hired Ballard Partners in late 2025 or early 2026. In just the first three months of 2026, Reliance reported paying the firm $120,000 (about ₹1 crore). The disclosure form said the target was the Department of Commerce and the issue was "international energy policy".

> Why is the Commerce Department important? Because its secretary is Howard Lutnick. Before joining the government, Lutnick was the CEO of Cantor Fitzgerald – the very same Wall Street firm that advised America First Refining on the Reliance deal. Lutnick stepped down from Cantor when he took office, and his family now runs the firm. Whether Reliance's lobbying ever touched on the refinery investment is not known – the public filings don't say.

#### The refinery's own lobbyists

While Reliance was targeting the Commerce Department, the struggling Texas refinery was also hiring its own influence machine. In 2025, Element Fuels (the project's name before it became "America First Refining") spent $450,000 (around ₹3.7 crore) on lobbying – its highest ever. The firm it hired was Continental Strategy, founded by Carlos Trujillo.

Trujillo is a former Trump administration official. He served as US Ambassador to the Organization of American States and as a special representative to the United Nations. Before that, he was a Florida state lawmaker and a Republican party delegate. His firm’s website promises "unparalleled access to key decision‑makers".

On disclosure forms, the stated goal of the lobbying was to talk to the President's office about "hydrogen technology". That sounds narrow and technical. But the Brownsville project is first and foremost a crude oil refinery, not a hydrogen plant. So the public filing may not tell the full story of what was actually discussed behind closed doors.

> #### A federal council as a sales agent?

John Calce, the refinery's CEO, publicly thanked the "National Energy Dominance Council" – a US government body – for helping him meet foreign investors. On a podcast, he said the Council "introduced us and helped us meet some of these people overseas". That means a federal agency was using taxpayer resources to help a bankrupt startup raise money from foreign sovereign wealth funds. Is that standard practice? The public record does not contain any rule book defining when and how the Council does this.

#### The bottom line

> The lobbying network around this deal had many layers: Reliance paying a Trump‑connected firm to target the Commerce Department; the refinery paying another Trump‑connected firm to target the President's office; and a former Trump official's firm getting hundreds of thousands of dollars. Meanwhile, the financial advisor for the deal was the former firm of the sitting Commerce Secretary. None of this is illegal by itself. But it shows how money, access, and policy can become intertwined – and how little of that interaction is required to be made public.

> #### The Interlocking Network

The lobbying network that surrounded the transaction had several layers, each with its own connections to the Trump administration.

> #### ***Could this be bribery under Indian or U.S. law?***

Under the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA), it is illegal to pay a foreign official to influence an official act. But Reliance invested in a private Texas company, not directly to any U.S. official. Donald Trump Jr., whose family LLC held a stake in that company, is a private citizen, not a government official. To prove an FCPA violation, prosecutors would need evidence that Reliance executives knew their money was meant to reach a U.S. official personally. No such evidence is public.

Under U.S. domestic bribery law, if the ₹800 crore investment was intended to influence the President to roll back tariffs or issue oil licenses, that could be a crime. But the investment went to a company, not to the President. Courts have sometimes treated payments to a public official's family as indirect bribes, but the Supreme Court has not ruled on this exact situation. Worse, a 2016 Supreme Court decision (McDonnell v. United States) made it much harder to prove bribery. It said that an "official act" must be a specific government decision — not just a meeting or a call. Even then, prosecutors must prove the official action happened *because* of the payment, not just after it. Without an email or a recording explicitly linking the investment to the policy changes, that is very hard to prove.

> #### **The biggest legal gap: the President is exempt**

There is a federal conflict of interest law that forbids government employees from working on matters that affect their own or their family's finances. But that law explicitly does not apply to the President of the United States. So the person with the most power over trade tariffs, sanctions, and oil licenses has no legal duty to recuse himself from decisions that could benefit his son's business interests. This is not a secret — it is the plain text of the law. ***Who and waht could actually be prosecuted then?***&#x20;

The most provable legal issue has nothing to do with bribery. It is about what the refinery company told its investors. America First Refining is a private company, but if it raised money from Reliance by hiding its CEO's history of bankruptcy and fake infrastructure claims, that could be securities fraud. John Calce's prior bankruptcy and the story of his fake company with phone numbers routed to a taxi service and a baklava caterer are documented facts. If Reliance was not told about them — and if those facts would have affected its investment decision — U.S. securities regulators could take action. But those disclosures are not public, so no one outside the deal knows what was said.

> #### **The bottom line**

U.S. law makes it very difficult to prosecute bribery when the money flows through a family member's company and the beneficiary is the President, who is exempt from conflict of interest rules. The system permits much of what happened here — unless someone finds a smoking gun email or recording. Whether that is how the law should work is a question for citizens and lawmakers, not just prosecutors.

What you read until now has laid out a clear chain of events. A foreign company facing heavy U.S. tariffs invested over ₹800 crore in a Texas refinery project that had a history of bankruptcy, permit troubles, and fake claims. The investment was structured through a secretive company registered at the home address of the US President’s son. The deal was announced after a high‑profile meeting in India between the President’s son and the heir of the Indian conglomerate. The law makes it very hard to prove a criminal deal. To prove bribery, you would need emails, recordings, or witnesses – none of which are public. The US President is exempt from conflict‑of‑interest laws that apply to other officials. The Supreme Court has also made it tougher to define an “official act” in bribery cases. So even if something looks wrong, it may not be punishable. But journalism’s job is not to send people to jail – it is to show the facts and let readers decide if the system is working.

> #### ***Six uncomfortable questions***

> **1. The President’s exemption.** The US President is the only top official who is not covered by the federal conflict‑of‑interest law. That means he can make trade and sanctions decisions that affect his own family’s business interests. Should the most powerful person in government be exempt from rules that apply to everyone else?
>
> **2. Hidden ownership.** The LLC that held the President’s son’s stake was registered in Wyoming, a state that does not require naming the real owners. Without a court order, no one knows exactly who owns what. Should companies that receive foreign money have to reveal their true owners?
>
> **3. Weak lobbying disclosures.** Lobbying firms filed forms saying they worked on “energy policy” or “hydrogen technology,” but those forms do not show what was actually discussed, who was met, or whether different firms coordinated. Is that enough transparency when billions are at stake?
>
> **4. The Texas permit extension.** Just days before the ₹800 crore investment was announced, a Texas state agency granted the refinery its third and final permit extension. The agency follows state law, not federal ethics rules. But the timing raises a question: was it a routine decision, or was political pressure involved?
>
> **5. A federal council as a sales agent.** The refinery’s CEO publicly said that a US government body – the National Energy Dominance Council – introduced his company to foreign investors. That would mean taxpayer‑funded officials were helping a bankrupt startup raise money. Is that normal? What are the rules?
>
> **6. No recusal from the Commerce Secretary.** The US Commerce Secretary used to run the very Wall Street firm that advised the refinery on the Reliance deal. His family now runs that firm. Meanwhile, his department was being lobbied by Reliance on “energy policy.” Did he step aside from those matters? No public record shows he did.

#### What the law does not forbid, it allows

Each of these points alone might seem like a small loophole. But together – in the context of ₹800 crore flowing from an Indian company to a politically connected American project, followed by policy changes worth billions to that same Indian company – they reveal a system full of gaps. The law does not stop a president’s son from holding a secret stake in a company that gets foreign money. It does not require the president to sit out decisions that help that company. It does not force lobbyists to fully disclose their talks. It does not prevent a state agency from fast‑tracking a permit. It does not stop a federal council from acting as a broker for a private firm. And it does not compel a cabinet secretary to recuse himself from matters involving his old firm.

In the end, what the law does not explicitly prohibit, it permits. Whether that is how a democracy should work is a question for every reader – and every voter.

This investigation has traced a documented chain of events: an Indian energy giant facing punitive US tariffs invested over ₹800 crore in a Texas refinery project with a history of bankruptcy and phantom infrastructure.
