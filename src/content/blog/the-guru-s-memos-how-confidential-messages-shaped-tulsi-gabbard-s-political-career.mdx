## &#x20;THE ANOMALY

> ### The Public Narrative vs. The Hidden Channel

Tulsi Gabbard, the former Democratic congresswoman from Hawaii who went on to become Donald Trump's Director of National Intelligence, has long presented herself as a political independent who answers to no one.

"I didn't always agree with Tulsi on everything," said Rebecca Saltzburg, who worked on digital strategy for several of Gabbard's congressional campaigns, in November 2024. "But as for the core of her life and political path? I can vouch 100 percent, that is her own."

Gabbard herself was emphatic when asked in 2019 whether her spiritual teacher, Chris Butler, had mentored her politically. "No, no, not at all," she said. (Source: Washington Post, citing 2019 interview)

But a trove of more than 25,000 pages of internal documents obtained by The Washington Post tells a different story.

The documents — confidential memos, emails, and transcripts — appear to document detailed political and policy guidance for Gabbard during her first two terms in Congress, from 2011 to 2017. They were sent through an encrypted email domain called NineIsles.com, which Saltzburg identified as belonging to Butler's office.

The memos contained instructions on what legislation Gabbard should propose, which policies she should embrace, and how she should conduct herself on television. Some were harshly critical. One memo from January 2015 quoted an unnamed adviser saying Gabbard had become ["really intellectually lazy.](https://www.washingtonpost.com/investigations/2026/06/21/tulsi-gabbard-her-guru-mysterious-messages-that-helped-shape-her-political-career/)" In another, she was described as ["chickenshit" and "mealymouthed](https://www.washingtonpost.com/investigations/2026/06/21/tulsi-gabbard-her-guru-mysterious-messages-that-helped-shape-her-political-career/)."&#x20;

The main speaker in these memos — the person issuing directives and sometimes castigating Gabbard — was never named. The memos were unattributed precisely to mask the speaker's identity if they ever became public, according to Saltzburg.

## THE EVIDENCE

> ### The Memos and the Public Record

The Washington Post investigation compared the content of the memos against Gabbard's public record and found unmistakable parallels.

* **Documented Example 1 — Veterans' Healthcare (2014):**

A memo labeled "Call with T" argued that to avoid delays in medical treatment, veterans should be able to get care at any hospital and be reimbursed by Veterans Affairs — and they should be able to do so without first obtaining government approval. "Actually put forward legislation. Get it done," the [unnamed speaker said.](https://www.washingtonpost.com/investigations/2026/06/21/tulsi-gabbard-her-guru-mysterious-messages-that-helped-shape-her-political-career/)&#x20;

Three weeks later, Gabbard endorsed a version of that policy in an op-ed in the Honolulu Star-Advertiser. But she omitted any discussion of prior approval — a key idea that distinguished the unnamed adviser's plan from remedies pushed by other Democrats.

Soon after the op-ed was published, Allison Hoen — identified as Butler's top aide — emailed Saltzburg: "He was pissed she didn't say that they should get private care withOUT pre-approval." (Source: Washington Post investigation)

A month later, Gabbard introduced a bill that made explicit that veterans would not need preapproval for private care.

* **Documented Example 2 — CNN Appearance (October 12, 2015):**

A memo labeled "CNN Wolf Blitzer Talking points (Final)" contained this language about reports that Gabbard had been asked by Democratic leadership not to attend a presidential debate: ["It's not a 'boohoo, I don't get to go to the party' situation, Wolf."](https://www.washingtonpost.com/investigations/2026/06/21/tulsi-gabbard-her-guru-mysterious-messages-that-helped-shape-her-political-career/)&#x20;

The Post dug up the clip of her appearance that day and found that she had used the line almost verbatim: "The issue here is not about me saying boo-hoo, I'm going to miss the party."&#x20;

* **Documented Example 3 — Syria Policy (August 2016):**

A memo documented tactical advice on one of Gabbard's signature policies: preventing the United States from ousting Syrian dictator Bashar al-Assad. The memo quoted an unnamed adviser saying she should reiterate her opposition to U.S. intervention in Syria's civil war. ["The CIA is the one that started this thing,"](<"The issue here is not about me saying boo-hoo, I'm going to miss the party.">) the person said.&#x20;

Gabbard made that claim publicly three years later. (Source: Washington Post investigation)

* **Documented Example 4 — The "Eye Thing":**

A June 2014 memo from Hoen to Gabbard ahead of a TV appearance was direct: "Don't forget to smile, etc. Don't do the eye thing."&#x20;

On YouTube, the Post found a clip of Gabbard on CNN the following day. At several points, she opened her eyes wide while speaking.

"Well, from what I saw, she's doing the f---ing eye thing again," an unnamed speaker told Hoen later that day, according to a transcript of their conversation. "She's still doing the eye thing."&#x20;

> ### The Pattern

The Post compared Gabbard's remarks in 32 TV interviews between 2014 and 2016 with the talking-points memos intended for them. On 24 occasions, Gabbard used language in the memos almost verbatim. In the eight other instances, Gabbard used different words but promoted some of the same ideas. (Source: Washington Post investigation)

**The Inference:** The available evidence suggests that someone outside Gabbard's official staff was providing her with detailed political guidance — and that she followed that guidance with remarkable consistency. The speaker in the memos, who remains unnamed in the documents, appears to have had significant influence over Gabbard's policy positions, media appearances, and legislative priorities.



## THE CONTRADICTIONS

> ### Who Was Behind the Memos?

***The central question of the investigation is the identity of the unnamed speaker.***

**The Public Claim:** Gabbard and her representatives have consistently denied that Butler played a political role. Butler himself told the New Yorker in 2017 that he did not tell Gabbard how to vote.

**The Documented Evidence:** The memos were sent through NineIsles.com, an email domain that Saltzburg identified as belonging to Butler's office. The memos were encrypted and unattributed — measures taken, according to Saltzburg, to mask Butler's identity.

**The Alternative Explanation:** Syd Khemaney, a senior disciple of Butler who worked with Gabbard, claimed that he, not Butler, was behind most of the memos. "The vast majority of these materials from more than a decade ago came from me and other advisers, including her father, State Sen. Mike Gabbard," [Khemaney told the Post.](https://www.washingtonpost.com/investigations/2026/06/21/tulsi-gabbard-her-guru-mysterious-messages-that-helped-shape-her-political-career/)&#x20;

1. **The Contradiction:** Khemaney's explanation is difficult to reconcile with several pieces of evidence:
2. **Stylometric Analysis:** The Post asked an AI chatbot to perform a stylometric analysis comparing the memos to Butler's lecture archive and to Khemaney's and Mike Gabbard's writings. The analysis suggested that the spoken remarks in the memos appeared to have been produced by just one person — and that the speaker was much more likely Butler than Khemaney or Mike Gabbard.&#x20;
3. **Linguistic Markers:** The analysis noted that "duplicity," a nonword used instead of "duplicitous," appeared once in the memos and nine times in Butler's lecture archive. Other nonstandard words used in both the memos and the lectures, including "judgmentalism," suggested Butler was the unnamed adviser.&#x20;
4. **Direct Self-Identification:** In 19 memos, the adviser appeared to identify himself as Butler — primarily in discussions about Gabbard's faith, where the identity of her guru was an inevitable talking point. Nothing in those documents distinguished them from the hundreds of others where the unnamed adviser did not self-identify.&#x20;
5. **Separate Identities:** One memo about a SIF legal issue was addressed from "Syd" (Khemaney's nickname) to "S" (Butler's nickname), indicating they are different people. Other documents were labeled as coming "from CP" — an abbreviation of "Car Port," a nickname for the house where Butler lived.
6. **Historical Pattern:** A family member of one of the candidates who had run for Independents for Godly Government in 1976 — a political party that sprang up in Hawaii with Butler followers — told the Post that Butler had advised the candidates while working to hide his connections to them, avoiding written communications and using intermediaries to deliver his campaign advice. She said Butler was obsessed with politics. He wanted, she said, "to rule the world."&#x20;

**The Unknowns:** What remains unproven is whether Butler was the primary author of the political memos. Khemaney's claim that he was the author cannot be definitively disproven without access to internal communications or testimony from those involved. The anonymity in the memos was deliberate, making definitive attribution impossible from the documents alone.

## &#x20;THE LEGAL AND SYSTEMIC QUESTIONS

> ### What This Reveals About the Boundaries of Political Influence

The Gabbard investigation raises several questions that transcend any single politician.

**Question 1: The Transparency Gap**

The memos were sent through an encrypted email domain, with attachments encrypted, and the speaker's identity deliberately obscured. This level of operational security suggests a conscious effort to avoid public scrutiny.

**The Question:** Should political advisors — whether they are official staff or unofficial influencers — be required to disclose their roles? What mechanisms exist to ensure transparency in political guidance?

**Question 2: The Religious Freedom Defense**

When the Post reached out to Gabbard's representatives, her spokeswoman urged the newspaper to drop the story. "I cannot imagine WaPo's readers would be interested in yet another uncredible, bigoted attack on the DNI's faith," she wrote.&#x20;

A vice president at a public relations firm representing Butler's organization provided a statement: "Hinduphobia, anti-Hindu religious bigotry, that's all this is. When a Hindu public figure has a spiritual teacher or shares views with a Hindu religious figure, that alone is somehow evidence of sinister control."

**The Question:** Where is the line between legitimate religious practice and undue political influence? Does the fact that a politician's spiritual advisor is also providing political guidance constitute a conflict of interest — or is it a protected expression of faith?

**Question 3: The Source Credibility Problem**

The primary source for the memos, Rebecca Saltzburg, had a complicated history with Butler's organization. After her arrest in a teenage-runaway case, she had demanded $250,000 from SIF and threatened reputational damage if she did not receive the money, according to a PR representative for the organization.&#x20;

Saltzburg acknowledged the demand but said it was to cover damages to her children and herself after she was left to face a felony charge. Texas prosecutors eventually declined to pursue a criminal case against Saltzburg, concluding they had insufficient evidence.&#x20;

> ***The Question:** Does Saltzburg's history undermine the credibility of the documents she provided? Or does the fact that the organization did not question the authenticity of the documents suggest they are genuine?*

## WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN

The following questions cannot be answered by the public record:

* **Did Gabbard actively seek the guidance documented in the memos, or was it imposed on her?**
* **Was Gabbard aware that the guidance was being documented and distributed?**
* **Did Gabbard ever refuse to follow the guidance documented in the memos?**
* **What was the full extent of Butler's involvement in Gabbard's political decision-making?**
* **Did the pattern of guidance continue after Gabbard left Congress and joined the Trump administration?**
* **What was discussed in the private conversations that were not documented?**
* **Did Gabbard's official staff know about the memos and the commenting network?**

##

This investigation has laid out a documentary record. It has shown what is proven: the memos, the encrypted email domain, the instructions on legislation and media appearances, the fake social media accounts, and the chronological alignment between the guidance and Gabbard's public actions.

It has shown what is contradictory: Gabbard's public denials of Butler's political role versus the documented evidence of guidance; the claim that she was a freethinker who took orders from no one versus the memos that told her what to say and do.

It has shown what remains unknown: who exactly was behind the memos, whether Gabbard followed the guidance willingly or reluctantly, and whether the pattern continued after she left Congress.

The reader is left to draw conclusions from the facts presented. The final question is not whether Gabbard received guidance from someone outside her official staff — the documents suggest she did. The final question is whether the system that permitted this hidden channel of influence to operate is the system the public believes it has.
