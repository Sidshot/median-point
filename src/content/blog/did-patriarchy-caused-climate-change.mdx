I watched the clip, unsatisfied and with more question I ended up watching [the parent podcast](https://youtu.be/CDimTbGwlsU]\(https://youtu.be/CDimTbGwlsU) where the reel was from . I would request you to watch both things before scrolling further.  In the podcast Dia Mirza declare&#x64;**, "Patriarchy caused the climate crises."**    (remember this line, these are her words and my main issue is with the way it was framed by her). In the aftermath of criticism, she clarified that she was referring not to men as individuals but to patriarchal systems—systems that, in her view, prioritise extraction over care, domination over stewardship, and profit over sustainability.

This clarification deserves to be taken seriously. But it also deserves to be questioned.

<Instagram url="https://www.instagram.com/reel/DZpHZ_Mo1rm/" />

Because somewhere between criticising systems of power and assigning moral responsibility for a planetary crisis, an important distinction has been blurred. When public figures proclaim that "men drove climate change" or that patriarchy is solely responsible for environmental destruction, they are not merely advancing an academic theory. They are making a political argument “a sweeping one” with consequences for how millions of people understand responsibility, guilt, and collective action.

This is where I part ways with Dia Mirza.

<YouTube id="https://youtu.be/Auh80oi1ZJ0" />

I have no quarrel with the proposition that exploitative institutions have damaged both societies and ecosystems. History offers ample evidence that industrial greed, unrestrained extraction, and short term political thinking have contributed significantly to the climate crisis. Nor do I deny that many societies historically concentrated political and economic power in male hands. **But** moving from these observations to the assertion that patriarchy caused the climate crisis—or that men, as a class, bear primary responsibility for it – this is where we need to walk carefully.

Climate change is arguably the most complex challenge humanity has ever faced. It is the cumulative result of industrialisation, fossil fuel dependence, geopolitical competition, consumer demand, economic systems geared towards endless growth, and policy failures spanning **centuries**. It is a story involving empires, corporations, governments, technologies, and billions of human choices across nations and generations. To compress that history into a gendered narrative may be rhetorically powerful, but it risks sacrificing accuracy for ideology. More importantly, it risks turning a universal human challenge into a moral contest between groups. Humans are emotional beings; it matters how you speak in public. Public language matters. Regardless of scholarly intentions, statements such as *"men caused climate change"* are not heard by most people as critiques of institutions. They are heard as collective blame. They tell ordinary men  many of whom are scientists, conservationists, engineers, activists, and citizens deeply committed to climate action that *they are somehow inheritors of an original environmental sin*. This is NOT fair and politically unwise. (and after reading for whole day on this I am certain that Dia really did not put a thought on this)

The climate movement requires the broadest coalition in human history. It demands cooperation across genders, classes, nations, and ideologies. Framing the crisis through the lens of collective gender guilt risks weakening that coalition by substituting shared responsibility with shared accusation. The purpose of my scribbling on paper and typing frantically on my laptop, therefore, is not to deny the role of power structures in shaping environmental outcomes. Nor is it to dismiss feminist perspectives on ecology outright. Rather, *it is to examine a specific claim critically: that patriarchy or by implication, men is the primary cause of climate change.*

I will argue that this claim is historically incomplete, empirically weak, and politically counterproductive. The real drivers of climate change lie elsewhere: in industrial systems, economic incentives, geopolitical rivalries, and patterns of consumption that transcend gender.

*If we are serious about saving the planet, we should be equally serious about describing the problem accurately. And accuracy demands that we resist explanations that are emotionally satisfying but analytically insufficient.*

 

#### **Why are people so divided on this issue?**

What exactly do we mean when we say "patriarchy caused the climate crisis"? If the statement means that historically male dominated institutions often prioritised economic expansion, resource extraction, and industrial growth over environmental concerns, then there is room for discussion. Historians, political scientists, and environmental scholars have debated this relationship for decades. But if the statement is intended or *even widely understood* to mean that men, as a category of people, are responsible for climate change, then the argument quickly runs into serious intellectual, moral difficulties, mudslinging in comment sections and just divisive discourse which defeats the purpose of the statement to begin with – ironical right?

Now this distinction is not semantic nitpicking. It is fundamental. Institutions are not biological entities, they are systems of incentives, laws, norms, economic arrangements, and political choices. Individuals operate within these systems, often constrained by circumstances far larger than themselves.

Allow me to simplify this with one example   the **Industrial Revolution**   the historical turning point that set humanity on its fossil fuel trajectory. Was it driven by men? *Certainly, many of the industrialists, politicians, and inventors of the era were male.*

 *But was their maleness the reason coal powered factories spread across Britain and then across the world?*

 *Or was it the enormous economic advantages that fossil fuels offered?*

**The answer is obvious**. Coal did not become dominant because men preferred it. It became so because it was *cheap, abundant, and extraordinarily efficient* at powering industrial economies. Steam engines did not conquer the world because of masculinity. They conquered it because they transformed productivity. The same logic applies to oil. The global petroleum economy expanded because nations required energy, industries demanded fuel, and societies embraced unprecedented levels of consumption. *The incentives driving this expansion were economic and geopolitical—not chromosomal.*

This is why blaming climate change **primarily** to patriarchy risks confusing who held power with what incentives shaped that power. The distinction is crucial. Suppose tomorrow every CEO of every major oil company were replaced by women. Suppose every parliament in the world instantly became majority female.

 

·       Would fossil fuels immediately disappear?

·       Would global emissions collapse overnight?

Almost certainly not. Because the underlying structures which are energy demand, industrial dependence, economic competition, consumer expectations—would still exist. Point that I am trying to make is that “the climate crisis is sustained by systems that transcend gender.”

This is not to deny history. *Men have indeed occupied the overwhelming majority of political and economic leadership positions throughout much of modern history*. That fact is undeniable. But **correlation is not causation**. To say that men historically held power is one thing. To conclude that masculinity itself caused climate change is another altogether.

The distinction becomes even more apparent when you examine empirical evidence. Consider Germany under Angela Merkel. A woman not merely a female leader; one she was a trained physicist, two, a former Environment Minister, and media called her "Climate Chancellor." Yet despite her environmental credentials and sixteen years in power, Germany remained heavily dependent on fossil fuels and coal. In 2021, the year Merkel left office, coal still accounted for roughly 28 percent of Germany's electricity generation. Germany's greenhouse gas emissions stood at approximately 762 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent. (take that!) Ambitious climate targets repeatedly collided with industrial interests, energy security concerns, and political realities. Merkel's gender did not exempt her government from the structural pressures of a modern industrial economy.

Now consider Margaret Thatcher, Britain's first female Prime Minister. Ironically, Thatcher was one of the earliest world leaders to warn publicly about climate change. In a 1989 speech to the United Nations, she warned that humanity was conducting "a massive experiment with the system of this planet itself." *Yet her government simultaneously presided over the rapid exploitation of North Sea oil and gas reserves*. *The United Kingdom's oil production surged during her premiership, and revenues from fossil fuels became a crucial pillar of Britain's economy*. Environmental awareness coexisted with fossil fuel dependence because economic incentives proved stronger than rhetoric.

For another one; take Norway, another revealing example; this country consistently ranks among the world's most gender equal countries. According to the Global Gender Gap Index, it has remained near the top for decades. Women occupy key positions in politics and business, and gender equality is deeply institutionalised. Yet it is also one of the world's largest exporters of oil and natural gas. Petroleum accounts for around half of Norway's exports and contributes significantly to government revenues.

> *Gender equality did not eliminate fossil fuel extraction because the incentives driving that extraction are economic and geopolitical, not biological*

These examples point to a broader truth. Political leaders, regardless of gender, govern within institutional constraints. They face economic pressures, electoral incentives, geopolitical realities, and competing interests. Their decisions are shaped by structures. And when you reduce this spiralling structure to sex, you either have vested interest or you are just ignorant by choice.

Let’s say **If** climate change were fundamentally the product of male identity, then replacing male leaders with female leaders should have produced dramatically different outcomes across countries and industries. The historical record does not support such a conclusion. What it shows instead is that leaders ‘men and women alike’ operate within systems of energy production, economic growth, and political incentives that often reward environmentally damaging choices.

The problem, therefore, is not masculinity. The problem is the set of institutions, incentives, and economic structures that humanity collectively built and continues to sustain.

 

There is another problem with collective blame. It ignores the millions of men who have dedicated their lives to environmental protection. The climatologists who sounded the alarm. The scientists who built climate models. The engineers designing renewable technologies. The activists risking arrest to oppose destructive projects. The conservationists protecting forests and ecosystems. To tell these individuals that they belong to the gender responsible for climate catastrophe is not a serious analysis of power and utter disregard for their hard work. It is a moral generalisation. And moral generalisations, however emotionally satisfying they may be, rarely produce good politics. Because climate change is not a male problem. It is not a female problem. It is not the consequence of one sex triumphing over another. It is a human problem—born from humanity's extraordinary capacity to innovate, consume, expand, and exploit without fully reckoning with the consequences.

 

#### **What it is then!**

If we are serious about assigning responsibility for climate change, then we must begin with history, not ideology. The climate crisis did not emerge because one gender collectively chose to dominate nature. It emerged because modern civilisation, across continents and political systems, embraced an economic model powered overwhelmingly by fossil fuels. From the late eighteenth century onward, coal transformed Britain. It fuelled factories, railways, steel production, and unprecedented economic growth. Soon, Europe followed. Then the United States. Later, the Soviet Union, China, India, Japan, and virtually every industrialising nation. The logic was brutally simple: cheap energy meant greater productivity; greater productivity meant economic and military power. No government wanted to be left behind. This does not sound masculine, more like a geopolitical one!

Countries competed. Industries expanded. Populations grew. Standards of living rose. Entire societies—men and women alike—benefited from the conveniences produced by carbon intensive growth. That fridge in your home, the electricity powering a shopping mall, the fertilisers increasing corn yield for your caramel popcorn, that airplane you take for concert  all of these emerged from the same industrial ecosystem that also accelerated greenhouse gas emissions. This historical reality complicates simplistic narratives of blame. If patriarchy alone caused climate change, then how do we explain the fact that communist states and capitalist states alike became major emitters? The former Soviet Union was not organised around free market capitalism, yet it became one of the largest emitters of greenhouse gases in history. Modern China officially rejects many traditional Western political structures, yet today it is among the world's largest carbon emitters. India, Brazil, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia—countries with vastly different political cultures and gender dynamics—have all pursued industrial expansion because economic development remains a national imperative.

Do you see the pattern? The principal driver is not gender. It is industrial modernity itself. More specifically, it is the combination of economic systems that reward continuous growth, energy systems historically dependent on coal, oil, and gas, political systems that prioritise short term prosperity over long term sustainability, global competition that encourages extraction rather than restraint, and consumer cultures that equate progress with ever increasing consumption. These forces operate across societies irrespective of who occupies positions of power. Blaming MEN for this shows frustration, it can help you score some brownie points, few likes on Instagram, a day at headline in The Hindu, a week of clout, a month of viral fever but NOT something concrete.

Consider another uncomfortable question.

If tomorrow every political office, every corporate boardroom, and every financial institution were staffed exclusively by women, would the pressures of economic growth disappear? Would governments stop worrying about employment? Would developing countries voluntarily abandon industrialisation? Would consumers suddenly reject modern comforts?

Again, the answer is obvious. The incentives would remain. The structures would remain. And the environmental challenges would remain. I am not making a cynical argument here. It is simply an acknowledgment that systems shape behaviour far more powerfully than identities alone.

YES, history offers examples that challenge gender essentialist assumptions. Women have led major industrial economies. Women have governed countries dependent on fossil fuels. Women have run large corporations, including energy companies. Their presence in leadership is an important achievement for equality. But it has not magically dissolved the political and economic realities surrounding climate policy. Nor should anyone has expected it to. Because environmental degradation is not the inevitable consequence of male identity. It is the consequence of incentives that reward extraction, consumption, and growth while externalising environmental costs.

This distinction matters because it changes where we direct our anger. If men are the problem, then social antagonism becomes the solution. But if institutions, incentives, and economic structures are the problem, then reform—not resentment—becomes the priority. And reform is far harder. It requires changing energy systems, reimagining growth, restructuring markets, and creating political coalitions capable of sustaining long term environmental policies. These tasks are immensely difficult. This is why narratives of blame are so tempting. They offer a villain. They simplify complexity. They provide moral certainty. It gives us an escape clause. “Look, men are the problem, if they were not here, we would have been better”. This resentment is easy to come by. People love to blame and hate to come together for solution.

Climate change is not a morality play with heroes and villains divided neatly along gender lines. It is the consequence of human ambition; an ambition shared, encouraged, and benefited from by societies as a whole. And unless we understand that uncomfortable truth, we risk fighting one another while the crisis itself continues to deepen.

 

 

#### **Then why are people agreeing with Dia Mirza?**

To understand why the phrase "Patriarchy caused the climate crises" resonates with so many people, one must understand the intellectual tradition from which it emerges. Time for some feminist lesson.

> “***Ecofeminism***”  At its strongest, ecofeminism offers a compelling critique. *It argues that the same mindset that historically treated women as subordinate also tended to treat nature as a resource to be controlled, exploited, and extracted from.*

According to this view, hierarchies of domination—man over woman, human over nature, coloniser over colony—share a common logic and I agree with it. History is replete with examples of exploitative economic systems devastating ecosystems while simultaneously marginalising vulnerable groups. Colonial empires extracted resources from lands with little regard for ecological balance. Industrial capitalism often pursued growth with scant regard for environmental costs. In many societies, women were excluded from political and economic decision making. These are serious historical realities.

But acknowledging these realities is not the same as accepting the far broader proposition that patriarchy is the primary cause of climate change. This is where the theory begins to encounter difficulties. Because once the argument shifts from "certain systems of domination contributed to environmental destruction" to "patriarchy caused the climate crisis," it risks becoming less an explanation and more a grand narrative—one that struggles to account for complexity, exceptions, and competing causes.

>  *Climate change is a global phenomenon spanning centuries, political systems, cultures, and economic arrangements. To attribute such an intricate historical process primarily to patriarchy is to elevate one explanatory framework above all others, often at the expense of factors that are equally, if not more, significant: industrialisation, fossil fuel dependence, technological development, geopolitical rivalry, and global consumption patterns.*

Even the feminist themselves has problem with the theory and this concern has been raised repeatedly. Many scholars have criticised strands of ecofeminism for what is called essentialism—the tendency to portray women as naturally caring, nurturing, and environmentally conscious, while associating men with domination, aggression, and exploitation. At first glance, this may appear flattering toward women. But it carries a troubling implication. If women are inherently closer to nature, then are they not also being confined to a stereotypical role? If men are naturally more exploitative, then are they not being reduced to their gender in exactly the way feminism historically resisted? Do you see the irony here?

After all, history does not support such neat divisions. Women too have participated in systems of extraction. Women have governed empires. Women have led corporations. Women have defended industrial expansion when political or economic interests demanded it. Conversely, men have been among the most prominent voices in environmental conservation, ecological science, and climate activism. Human beings are capable of both care and exploitation. Neither virtue nor vice belongs exclusively to one sex. The environmental record of societies is not determined by the gender of those who lead them alone, but by the institutions they inherit, the incentives they face, and the choices they collectively make.

This becomes important when public intellectuals or so called celebrities translate academic theories into slogans. Academic theories often rely on nuance, qualifications, and carefully defined concepts. Public discourse rarely does. When an academic paper critiques "patriarchal structures," readers may understand that it refers to institutional arrangements developed over centuries. But when a public figure says, "Patriarchy caused climate change,"  a doomscroller on Instagram hears "Men caused this."

And that is precisely where communication becomes a political issue. Because whatever the intention behind the statement, public language does not exist in a vacuum. It shapes perceptions. It creates allies—or opponents. It frames how people understand responsibility. If a message intended to criticise institutions is widely understood as condemning individuals, then surely it is reasonable to question whether that message is helping the cause it seeks to advance. This is not merely a question of feelings. It is a question of political strategy.

Climate action requires unprecedented cooperation. It requires citizens across ideological, cultural, and gender lines to support difficult economic and social transitions. That coalition becomes harder to build when the rhetoric surrounding climate change evolves from shared responsibility into collective accusation. The danger here is not that ecofeminism raises uncomfortable questions. The danger is that complex systems are reduced to simple identities. That historical processes become moral judgments. That structural critique becomes gender blame.

*Because once politics begins assigning guilt to entire groups rather than examining institutions and incentives, it ceases to illuminate reality.* It begins to simplify it. And climate change is far too serious a crisis to be approached with comforting simplifications. The world does not face an environmental catastrophe because one sex chose destruction while the other chose care. The world faces an environmental catastrophe because humanity, as a civilisation, built extraordinary prosperity on systems whose environmental costs we ignored for far too long.

That is a tragedy. But it is also an important distinction. Because if the problem is humanity's institutions, then everyone has a role in changing them. If the problem is a gender, then politics becomes an exercise in blame. And blame has never reduced a single tonne of carbon dioxide.

 

#### **The Politics of Collective Guilt and TRAP**

There is a reason why phrases such as "men caused climate change" provoke such intense reactions. The issue is not merely whether the statement is academically defensible. It is whether such rhetoric is politically wise. I would argue that it is not. Because collective guilt is a dangerous foundation on which to build any mass movement.

Modern democratic politics functions on coalition building. Climate action, perhaps more than any other cause in history, requires broad and sustained cooperation. Governments must act. Businesses must adapt. Citizens must change habits. Scientists must innovate. Consumers must make difficult choices. Entire societies must participate. In such a context, rhetoric matters enormously. Language that frames climate change as a shared human challenge encourages collective responsibility. Language that frames it as the fault of one group risks producing the exact opposite: defensiveness, resentment, and division.

Imagine telling a young male climate scientist dedicating his career to renewable energy that his gender is historically responsible for planetary destruction. Imagine telling a male environmental activist who has spent years campaigning against coal plants that he is part of the problem because of an inherited social identity. Imagine telling ordinary men—many of whom possess no political power whatsoever—that they bear moral responsibility for centuries of industrial decisions made by elites they neither elected nor benefited from equally. Would this inspire solidarity? Or would it breed alienation? The answer seems self evident.

Political movements succeed not by expanding circles of blame but by expanding circles of responsibility. The civil rights movement did not succeed by condemning every individual belonging to a racial group. Anti colonial movements did not succeed by assigning eternal guilt to entire populations. Successful democratic movements identify unjust systems while inviting broad participation in reform. This distinction is crucial. There is a profound difference between saying, "Historically, institutions dominated by men often excluded women and pursued exploitative policies," and saying, "Patriarchy caused climate change." The first statement invites discussion. The second risks becoming a moral indictment. And moral indictments have a peculiar habit of oversimplifying history.

Because who exactly is being blamed? The farmer in rural India who emits a fraction of the carbon of a Western consumer? The young man born today, inheriting a world he did not create? The millions of ordinary men struggling with rising temperatures, floods, droughts, and pollution just as women are? Climate change does not discriminate between chromosomes. Heatwaves do not. Floods do not. Cyclones do not. Entire communities—men, women, and children alike—bear the consequences. Which is why turning climate politics into a contest of gendered guilt appears not merely inaccurate but strategically self defeating.

There is another reason to be cautious. History is full of examples where societies attempted to explain complex crises by locating blame in a single group. Economic collapses were blamed on minorities. Social decline was blamed on immigrants. Crime was blamed on particular communities. Rarely did such narratives solve the underlying problem. More often, they created new divisions while leaving old structures untouched. The climate crisis is too important to repeat that mistake.

Because the greatest beneficiaries of gender wars are often the institutions most responsible for environmental destruction. When public debate becomes men versus women, attention shifts away from the fossil fuel companies that lobbied against climate action, the governments that delayed transition for political convenience, the corporations that externalised environmental costs, and the economic incentives rewarding unsustainable growth. The systems remain intact. The arguments become cultural. And the emissions continue.

That is perhaps the greatest irony of all. The more climate discourse becomes centred on identity and blame, the less attention is devoted to the structures that actually require transformation. A coal plant does not care about gender. An oil field does not emit less carbon because it is managed by a man or a woman. Atmospheric chemistry is indifferent to identity politics. Carbon dioxide accumulates regardless of ideology.

Which is why I remain deeply sceptical of narratives that seek villains in demographics rather than institutions. The climate crisis is not a war between men and women. It is a struggle between sustainable and unsustainable systems. And confusing the two may be emotionally satisfying. But it is unlikely to save the planet.

#### **What do we need then?**

If I have spent the preceding sections criticising Dia Mirza's statement, it is not because I believe gender is irrelevant to climate politics. It is not. Women, particularly in poorer and climate vulnerable regions, often bear disproportionate burdens from environmental degradation. Droughts affect access to water, crop failures increase food insecurity, and displacement caused by floods and extreme weather can expose women to serious economic and social vulnerability. These realities deserve serious attention. Diverse perspectives can improve policymaking, broader participation can produce more equitable outcomes, and the historical exclusion of women from many political and economic institutions is a legitimate issue that democratic societies should continue addressing. **None of this is controversial**. What is controversial—and *what should remain controversial—is the attempt to convert these observations into a sweeping theory of guilt*.

Because there is a profound difference between saying, "Women are disproportionately affected by climate change," and saying, "Men are responsible for climate change." The first is an empirical observation. The second is a political accusation. And political accusations demand a far higher standard of evidence than rhetorical applause. This is where I believe many contemporary discussions lose their footing. Modern public discourse increasingly favours explanations that divide society into moral categories: oppressors and oppressed, villains and victims, guilty groups and innocent groups. These frameworks can sometimes illuminate injustice, but they can also oversimplify reality. Climate change is perhaps the clearest example of why.

 

#### **Where do this lead us?**

This debate surrounding Dia Mirza's statement is ultimately about how we understand the climate crisis itself. I reject explanations that reduce centuries of industrialisation, economic growth, geopolitical competition, and human ambition to the actions of one social group which is ironical in itself once you read her own explnation post. Climate change was not caused by men as a biological category, but by institutions that rewarded extraction, economic systems obsessed with growth, and societies that often prioritised prosperity over sustainability. This does not mean responsibility is evenly distributed, corporations, governments, and powerful elites must be held accountable but accountability does not mean collective guilt. I do not deny that patriarchal structures have shaped societies or that gender influences how people experience climate change. What I reject is the hyperbolic statements like her, from these observations to the claim that men caused climate change. Humanity already possesses a more powerful and unifying narrative: that we collectively created this crisis, collectively failed to act soon enough, and collectively bear the responsibility to change course. The climate crisis is vast enough without turning it into a gender war.&#x20;
