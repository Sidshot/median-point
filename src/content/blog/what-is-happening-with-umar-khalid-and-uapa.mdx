If you don’t know who Umar Khalid is, allow me to  give a brief summary of who he is and why his case is a curious one. He  is a former student leader from Jawaharlal Nehru University who became a prominent public figure during the 2016 JNU controversy and later participated in protests against the Citizenship Amendment Act (CAA). He was arrested in September 2020 in connection with the "larger conspiracy" case related to the 2020 Delhi riots and charged under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act (UAPA). Delhi Police alleges that he was involved in planning and coordinating activities that contributed to the riots, while Khalid denies the allegations and says he advocated only peaceful protest. Multiple courts have declined to grant him bail, holding that the prosecution's evidence establishes a prima facie case that warrants trial, though this does not amount to a finding of guilt. His supporters, civil-liberties groups, and several academics argue that the case is politically motivated and point to the fact that he has spent years in jail without the trial being completed. As of 2026, he remains in custody awaiting the conclusion of judicial proceedings.

Now that there is basic understanding of who he is, let me take you into the rabbit hole of his incarceration and his long tussle with the Indian legal system and tussle with the National government itself. Khalid is considered an Indian activist by the left wing whose public profile has long been defined by his abrasive, often incendiary rhetoric and his vocal dissent against the prevailing political climate (say right wing of Indian Politics). To many, he serves as a polarizing figure—an individual who engages in the "political theatre of outrage" and challenges state narratives with a language designed to provoke. However, his story has moved far beyond his personal ideological battles to become a significant, albeit troubling, case study in the vulnerability of democratic institutions.

His case is a curious and unsettling emblem of institutional overreach because it centers on a profound disconnect: the state’s aggressive use of incarceration without the corresponding weight of concrete evidence. While he is viewed by some as a reckless provocateur, the central issue is not his speech, but the legal machinery used to silence him. The state’s case appears to rest less on direct, actionable evidence than on a "sprawling mosaic of inference," using silence, association, and ideological positioning as a basis for long-term detention. It raises a fundamental question for any democracy: can institutions retain their credibility when the line between law enforcement and political punishment blurs, and when the legal system allows the penalty of incarceration to be administered long before a verdict is ever reached? 

The prolonged incarceration of Umar Khalid, without conviction and without closure, has come to represent something larger than one man’s legal fate. It now stands as a troubling emblem of what happens when the machinery of the state begins to blur the line between law enforcement and political punishment. Whether one regards Khalid as a reckless provocateur or a committed ideological dissenter, the deeper question is not about his speeches alone. It is about whether a democracy can continue to call itself confident in its institutions when the punishment appears to arrive long before proof.

Khalid is not a sympathetic figure in the conventional sense. By any fair reading, he was often incendiary, deliberately so. He spoke in a language that seemed designed to provoke, and he appeared to understand the political theatre of outrage as well as any modern activist. That matters. Public speech has consequences, especially in a country as combustible as India. But consequences are not the same as criminal conspiracy, and rhetorical irresponsibility is not identical to terrorism. The state’s case has tried to bridge that gulf with a confidence that is, frankly, curious and to some degree as the left argues arm twisting dissent against the government. The core concern is not merely the gravity of the charges, but the quality of the evidence used to sustain them. A publicly accessible speech, social media association without meaningful participation, and statements from co-accused persons under obvious pressure do not, by themselves, settle the question of guilt. They may create suspicion. They may support an inquiry. They do not automatically justify a legal architecture that allows incarceration to become its own form of punishment. When the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act (UAPA) is used in a manner that makes trial secondary to detention, the law ceases to function as a shield for the republic and begins to resemble a blunt instrument of state power.

To understand why this case acquired such political temperature, YOU MUST return to the anxieties surrounding the Citizenship Amendment Act and the wider fear of the CAA-NRC-NPR nexus. For many “Muslims”, the issue was not simply one statute or one administrative register. It was the possibility that citizenship could become conditional in a new and asymmetrical way: protected for some, precarious for others. It emerged from a climate already charged by official rhetoric, by the memory of the Assam NRC, and by the widespread perception that a bureaucratic test of belonging could, in practice, become a tool of exclusion. That context does not excuse reckless activism. It does, however, explain why the atmosphere became so febrile. In that environment, political language hardened, suspicion multiplied, and campuses began to reflect the larger national crisis of trust. Khalid and his allies moved within that atmosphere, often irresponsibly, sometimes provocatively, and at times with astonishing poor judgment. But bad judgment is not proof of orchestration. A democracy of one billion plus citizens that cannot distinguish between the two is already in trouble.

There is also the larger problem of selective severity. The public has been asked to accept a story in which a man supposedly masterminded riots through silence, association, and inference, while figures with more direct proximity to the violence were treated with a far softer institutional touch. That asymmetry is difficult to ignore. In any serious legal system, the proximity of an accused person to the material machinery of violence ought to matter. The gathering of weapons, the distribution of funds, the tactical use of rooftops, the presence of a mob, the reported injuries and deaths that followed—these are not abstractions. They are the physical anatomy of violence. Yet when the legal narrative becomes politically useful, the state’s attention can drift toward ideological targets even when more concrete actors remain closer to the scene.This is where the case against Khalid becomes especially unsettling. Its architecture appears to rest less on direct action than on a conspiracy theory stretched across speeches, group memberships, meetings, and insinuations. The result is not a clear chain of proof but a sprawling mosaic of inference. And inference, however seductive, is not the same as evidence tested in the open. If the prosecution’s case requires the public to believe that silence inside a chat group outweighs the presence of arms, or that ideological disagreement is itself a form of violence, then the legal threshold has been lowered to a dangerous degree.

One must also reckon with the ideological baggage embedded in Khalid’s politics. His references to Afzal Guru, his proximity to the language of Muslim grievance, and his tendency to situate state action within a frame of systemic oppression have alienated many who might otherwise have been willing to hear a more sober critique. That alienation is real. It should not be denied or softened. But there is a difference between offensive political symbolism and criminal conspiracy. The first may be repulsive, reckless, or politically ruinous. The second requires proof.What this case finally reveals is not simply the vulnerability of one activist. It reveals the vulnerability of institutions to their own tempter: the temptation to substitute political certainty for judicial restraint. A state that prosecutes ideology as if it were a weapon begins to lose sight of the fact that liberal democracy is meant to protect even those whose views are disagreeable, abrasive, or deeply unfashionable. Once that principle is weakened, it becomes easy to imagine that the law is being used against the dangerous few, when in fact it is being reshaped for everyone.Khalid may well remain, in the public mind, an abrasive and polarizing figure. That is not the issue. The issue is whether the state can justify years of incarceration without the clarity that justice requires. If the answer depends on stretched inferences, untested claims, and the punitive logic of endless detention, then the damage is no longer limited to one accused man. It extends to the republic itself.

It is that citizens begin to lose faith in the idea that courts are places where evidence matters more than temperament, and law matters more than politics. That is a much larger loss.
