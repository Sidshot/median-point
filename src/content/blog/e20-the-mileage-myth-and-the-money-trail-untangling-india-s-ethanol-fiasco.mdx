In the last few months, E20 petrol has gone from a policy term most people barely noticed to a national argument splashed across social media, news debates, and automotive forums. On one side are official claims that ethanol blending is safe, strategic, and good for India’s energy future. On the other side are drivers, engineers, and skeptics pointing to mileage drops, compatibility concerns in older vehicles, hidden maintenance costs, and a rollout that feels far ahead of the public’s understanding of its risks. What makes the story more confusing is that the criticism is not coming only from random internet outrage. It is being built from technical studies, public disclosures, policy documents, and financial data that raise real questions about how this transition was designed, who benefited from it, and why dissent has been so aggressively dismissed.

This is an opinion piece, not a press note. I am going to unpack the E20 story piece by piece: what it is, how it started, how the rollout was accelerated, what the government says, what the technical documents actually suggest, and where the contradictions begin. The aim is simple: to separate noise from evidence and to make sense of the what, why, how, which, when, and who behind the ethanol fiasco.

### Here is where the questions begin.

Every major public policy eventually reaches a point where slogans stop working and evidence starts mattering.

The E20 rollout has reached that point.

So, instead of debating Twitter clips, WhatsApp forwards, or political speeches, let's examine the criticism one question at a time. Some concerns are exaggerated. Others deserve far more attention than they have received.

Let's begin with perhaps the most important question of them all.

---

### • If my pre-2023 car was only designed for E10, am I basically being forced to act as a beta tester for E20?

#### What we know

Let's start with something both supporters and critics of ethanol blending broadly agree on.

Vehicles manufactured before April 2023 were engineered around **E10**—petrol containing up to 10% ethanol. The government's own transition roadmap required manufacturers to begin producing E20-compatible vehicles only from 2023 onwards.

Now, this does **not** mean an older car suddenly becomes undriveable the moment E20 enters the tank. Engines do not mysteriously explode because ethanol content increased by ten percentage points.

The concern has always been something much less dramatic—and therefore much easier to dismiss in public debate.

E20 has stronger solvent properties than E10. It also absorbs moisture more readily from the atmosphere because ethanol is hygroscopic. Those two characteristics have been studied for years, both in India and internationally.

Don't take my word for it. Read the studies yourself.

### [1. ARAI Material Compatibility Study (India)]([https://www.team-bhp.com/forum/attachments/indian-car-scene/2638765d1723106554-list-all-cars-e20-e20-petrol-compliant-arai-journal-e20.pdf]\(https://www.team-bhp.com/forum/attachments/indian-car-scene/2638765d1723106554-list-all-cars-e20-e20-petrol-compliant-arai-journal-e20.pdf\))

**What did they find?**

> Among other observations, the study reported that NBR-PVC blends and Epichlorohydrin elastomers—the rubber compounds commonly used in older fuel systems—showed significant swelling and degradation under prolonged exposure. It also documented a thermomechanical exhaust valve failure in a BS-VI turbocharged engine after approximately 265 hours of testing.

---

### [2. SAE International Standard J1748]([https://www.sae.org/works/documentHome.do?comtID=TEVFSS\&docID=J1748_201808\&inputPage=dOcDeTaIlS]\(https://www.sae.org/works/documentHome.do?comtID=TEVFSS\&docID=J1748_201808\&inputPage=dOcDeTaIlS\))

**What did they find?**

> Testing conducted using SAE J1748 protocols consistently shows that increasing ethanol concentration from E10 to E20 changes the fuel's polarity. In practical terms, ethanol becomes a more aggressive solvent, capable of extracting plasticizers from legacy rubber components, eventually making hoses and seals brittle as they age.

---

### [3. NREL / U.S. EPA Mid-Level Ethanol Studies]([https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/2014/03/f12/ft_05_knoll.pdf]\(https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/2014/03/f12/ft_05_knoll.pdf\))

**What did they find?**

> Before allowing higher ethanol blends, the U.S. National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL), Department of Energy (DOE), and Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) spent years studying legacy fuel systems.
>
> Their findings repeatedly highlighted two issues.
>
> First, ethanol aggressively absorbs moisture from surrounding air. If a vehicle remains parked for extended periods, the absorbed water can eventually separate from petrol—a phenomenon known as **phase separation**—leaving a corrosive water-ethanol layer at the bottom of the fuel tank.
>
> Second, ethanol's solvent properties dissolve varnishes and accelerate deterioration of older rubber components that had remained stable under conventional petrol.
>
> Notice what these studies are **not** saying.
>
> None of them argue that E20 instantly destroys an engine.
>
> They point towards something more subtle: **certain materials found in older fuel systems are likely to experience accelerated wear compared to their behaviour under E10.**

That distinction matters.

So no—you are not a "beta tester" in the literal sense.

The laboratory testing has already been done.

The question is whether owners of vehicles designed around E10 are now expected to absorb the long-term consequences that those laboratory studies identified.

***The Government's Position***

The government strongly rejects the idea that E20 is harming vehicles.

In July 2026, Union Minister Nitin Gadkari publicly challenged critics by asking:

Those are engineering questions, and engineering questions are best answered with engineering data.

### ·       **Are the rubber hoses, gaskets, O-rings, and seals in my older vehicle actually designed to withstand the higher solvent properties of 20% ethanol?**

 

No! They are not. Vehicles manufactured prior to April 2023 were materially engineered to be compatible with a maximum of 10% ethanol (E10).

 Ethanol is an active solvent. When its concentration is doubled to 20%, it changes the polarity of the fuel.

Testing (the same reports you saw above) confirms that prolonged exposure to E20 causes legacy elastomers—specifically NBR-PVC blends and Epichlorohydrin rubbers commonly used in older fuel hoses, O-rings, and gaskets—to swell, lose tensile strength, and eventually harden or crack once the fuel evaporates. While the metallic engine block itself remains safe, these critical non-metallic seals will degrade noticeably faster than they would under E10. All these reports are unbiased and are based on hard science.

 

**So, what does govt says to this?**

Despite this admission, the government has refused to halt the mandate, with ministers aggressively defending the fuel's safety based on the lack of catastrophic metallic engine failures, while largely sidestepping the issue of accelerated wear on rubber lines. ###  ·       **What happens to my vehicle's fuel system over time when exposed to the moisture that ethanol naturally absorbs?**

 

Ethanol is highly hygroscopic— meaning it actively attracts and absorbs water from the humidity in the air. In a tropical, humid country like India (especially during the monsoons in cities like Mumbai, Chennai, or Delhi), this is a massive mechanical liability for cars that aren't driven every single day.

**How it affects your car?**

If your car sits idle for a few weeks, the ethanol in the E20 fuel absorbs moisture until it reaches a saturation point. Once saturated, the water-ethanol mixture chemically separates from the petrol. Because water is heavier, this highly corrosive mixture sinks directly to the bottom of your fuel tank. This is called Phase Separation.

Where does your car's fuel pump sit? Exactly at the bottom of the tank. When you finally start the car after a long trip, instead of pumping combustible petrol, your engine sucks up a corrosive water-ethanol sludge. Over time, this literally rusts metal fuel tanks from the inside out, clogs fuel filters with oxidized debris, and causes the engine to violently sputter, knock, or completely stall out on the highway.

### ·       **Will my engine start knocking, vibrating, and suddenly stall due to ethanol cleaning out old tank gunk and clogging the fuel pump?**

###  

Yes. This isn't a WhatsApp conspiracy; and if you have read this far I am sure you would not think so too.

It is a fundamental chemical reaction. Ethanol is a highly effective, aggressive industrial solvent.

 

> **How it affects your car?**

If you own a car that is a few years old, it has spent its life running on standard petrol (or E10). Over time, this leaves a natural build-up of varnish, sludge, and microscopic sediment at the bottom of the fuel tank.

When you introduce E20, the high ethanol concentration acts like a chemical degreaser. It actively strips years of accumulated gunk right off the inner walls of your fuel tank.

**Where does that dissolved sludge go?**

 It gets sucked straight into your fuel lines. Within a few tank cycles, this heavy debris severely clogs your fuel filter and blocks your engine's delicate fuel injectors.

 When injectors clog, your engine starves for fuel. The physical result is engine knocking, violent vibrations, sputtering, and sudden, dangerous stalling on that BIG LARGE BEAUTIFUL highway.

 

> **BUT The Govt says *“There has not been a single car that faced issues due to E20 petrol,&#x20;*[*this is a paid campaign!”*](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/india/serviced-vehicles-not-facing-difficulty-hardeep-puri-rejects-engine-damage-claims-over-e20-fuel/articleshow/132240920.cms)**

 

Well, global automotive engineering and basic chemistry do not agree with “this government”.

For example, when the United Kingdom transitioned to just E10 fuel, the RAC (Royal Automobile Club) and standard automotive bodies explicitly warned drivers of legacy cars that ethanol's solvent properties would dissolve old deposits in fuel tanks and lines, leading directly to clogged filters and poor running.

Automotive engineers globally recommend that when transitioning an older vehicle to high-ethanol blends, the fuel filters must be replaced frequently during the first few months to catch the loosened debris.

&#x20;You can read the RAC's technical warning on ethanol acting as a solvent [here](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://www.rac.co.uk/drive/advice/emissions/what-is-e10-fuel-and-how-will-it-affect-you/).
